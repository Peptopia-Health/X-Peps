The Queen Street Bottle

Identification & history guide — third edition, with the owner’s measured dimensions and a refined reading of the base · Tuscarora Arboretum, Artifact No. 1 · Dug at 117 South Queen Street, Martinsburg, West Virginia, October 25, 2026

A small glass flask came out of the Queen Street earth with a patent number on its base. This edition follows that number wherever it leads — including to the honest places where the trail runs out.

The Verdict, Up Front

Your bottle is a machine-made, unembossed druggist/toiletry stock bottle of the flat-oval panel family — small in the hand, at less than 4 inches tall, less than 3 inches wide, and about 1 inch thick (owner-measured) — sold empty by a glasshouse, filled and paper-labeled by a druggist or small maker — most plausibly made between about 1915 and 1928, and most likely in the 1920s. It most likely held a toilet preparation — hand or skin lotion, hair tonic, or toilet water — or a druggist’s own liquid preparation. Its maker is unknown: it carries no maker’s mark.

The base carries raised embossing that now reads, with high confidence from the owner’s straight-on black-and-white photograph, “U.S. PAT. 107917.” Every constructible reading of that string has been run to ground in the patent record — 107917 itself (an 1870 ship’s surge-reliever; a 1938 dress design) and the earlier candidate readings 1079017, 107907, and 10701 (a 1913 shoe-heel tool; an 1870 necktie and a 1938 embroidered-fabric design; an 1854 railroad car brake and an 1878 cassimere design) — and not one of them is a bottle patent. The marking therefore does not identify or date this bottle; the identification below rests on the glass, the finish, and the form, exactly as established bottle research prescribes. What the number most plausibly means is set out in §3 — labeled as inference, because that is what it is.

1 · The Find

On October 25, 2026, the bottle was dug from the ground at 117 South Queen Street in downtown Martinsburg — a lot inside the Downtown Martinsburg Historic District (National Register, 1980), in the Queen and King Street commercial core. It came out intact, heavily crusted, and was photographed as found. It was then gently cleaned — soft work, no harsh treatment — and photographed again. The cleaning changed the picture in three important ways, and the second edition corrected the first wherever the new photographs required it. This third edition adds the owner’s own measurements of the bottle and a refined, glyph-by-glyph reading of the base (§2, §3).

Corrections to the first edition — stated plainly

  • “Strong amethyst.” The first edition described deep purple glass. After cleaning, the tint is a faint lavender blush — most of the purple was burial crust and staining on the glass, not color in it.

  • Proportions. The bottle is squatter than the first edition implied.
  • “No embossing anywhere.” True of the body; the base, unreadable under crust in the first edition, carries the patent embossing.
  • The base. The first edition could only speculate about marks under the crust. The legible feature is the patent line; no Owens suction scar can be positively identified from the new photographs.
  • Size — now measured (third edition). No earlier edition measured the bottle. The owner, holding it, gives: less than 4 inches tall, less than 3 inches wide, about 1 inch thick, narrow neck (§2 table).
  • The base reading, settled (third edition). The owner’s straight-on black-and-white photograph reads the embossing as “U.S. PAT. 107917” — six digits, the loop a 9 and a final 7 at the rim — superseding the glare-limited 1079017 read and the five-digit 10701 grouping (§3).

3 · The Patent — the Centerpiece, Resolved Honestly

A patent number on a bottle base is normally a door: follow it, and you may find the designer, the maker, the date. This door was followed completely. Here is what is on the other side.

The current best reading — “U.S. PAT. 107917”

A later photograph settled what glare had left open. In the owner’s straight-on black-and-white photograph of the base, the letters U S P A T are all legible and the digits read 1 0 7 9 1 7 — the loop glyph resolving as a 9, with a final 7 visible at the rim: six digits. That is now the best reading, held with high confidence, and it supersedes both earlier reads — the seven-digit 1079017 of the first, glare-lit photographs and the owner’s five-digit 10701 grouping. It was run to ground like the others. As a utility patent, U.S. Patent 107,917 is John E. Jones of Waretown, New Jersey — “Improvement in Surge-Relievers,” an elastic anchor-cable apparatus for ships, patented October 4, 1870. As a design patent, Design Patent 107,917 is Jacob Abbate of New York — “Design for a Dress,” patented January 11, 1938. Neither is a bottle.

The reading history, in one place: every constructible reading of the base string has now been checked against the patent record — 107917 (1870 ship’s surge-reliever; 1938 dress design), 1079017 (1913 shoe-heel burnishing tool), 107907 (1870 necktie improvement; 1938 embroidered-fabric design), and 10701 (1854 railroad car-brake machine; 1878 cassimere fabric design) — and every one of them resolves outside bottles and glass. The searches are documented in full below. The mark therefore remains unattributable: a maker’s style or mold designation, or an unrecoverable glyph error, are the standing possibilities, taken up at the end of this section.

The first full reading — 1,079,017 — and what it actually is

Established fact (Google Patents and the official patent document): U.S. Patent 1,079,017 is a utility patent titled “Sectional heel-edge finishing and burnishing tool.” Its inventor was Carl A. Matson of Lynn, Massachusetts, who assigned a one-half interest to Albert M. Hoyt of Swampscott, Massachusetts. It was filed on March 10, 1913 and granted on November 18, 1913. It covers a rotary machine tool for finishing and burnishing the heels of boots and shoes. Its six claims concern a helical wire rim and pivoting metal blocks. It has nothing to do with glass, bottles, containers, or closures — and its drawings show a shoemaker’s machine, not a bottle.

The other readings — also checked, also ruled out

  • 1,079,017 with a dropped digit — “107907”: as a design patent, USD107907 is a “Design for an embroidered fabric” (Leo L. Kahn, granted January 11, 1938) — wrong subject, wrong decade. As a utility patent, US107907 is an 1870 “Improvement in neckties” (John G. Hitchcock). Neither can mark this bottle.
  • An earlier reading — “U.S. PAT. 10701”: from the first cleaned photographs, David read the string as five digits: 10701 — a grouping the later black-and-white photograph superseded. That reading was run to ground as well. As a utility patent, U.S. Patent 10,701 is Joseph Marks of Dunkirk, New York — “Machine for Operating Car-Brakes,” a railroad car-brake machine, patented March 28, 1854. As a design patent, Design Patent 10,701 is Frederick S. Bosworth of Providence, Rhode Island, assignor to the Weybosset Mills — “Design for Fancy Cassimeres,” a woven fabric pattern, patented May 28, 1878. Neither is a bottle. And the numbering itself argues against the reading as a live citation: in this bottle’s era, 1915–1928, current utility patents were seven-digit numbers (roughly 1.1 to 1.65 million) and design patents ran about 70,000–75,000 — a bare five-digit “10701” belongs to neither live series. So the conclusion holds on every reading tried: the base string is not a resolvable patent citation for this bottle; a maker’s style or mold designation, or glyphs still misread, remain the open possibilities.
  • The whole neighborhood: every U.S. utility patent from 1,079,000 through 1,079,030 — all granted the same day as 1,079,017 — was opened and read. Thirty-one patents: concrete culvert molds, brake shoes, igniters, water heaters, a pin, a cigar cutter-lighter, a window burglar alarm. Not one touches bottles, containers, closures, glass, perfume, or dispensing.
  • One-digit misreads: twelve variants a worn digit could produce (1,078,017; 1,079,117; 1,073,017; and so on) were checked. The only container patent among them is US1,029,017 (1912) — a two-compartment friction-top tin can for the American Can Company. A metal can’s patent does not go on a glass bottle.
  • The collector record: exact-phrase searches across the web, the antique-bottles.net forums, the glass-marks references, and sale listings found no bottle, product, or discussion anywhere linked to the number 1079017.
  • Design patents: a bottle-shape patent of this era would be a D-series design patent, and bottles were normally marked “DES.” with that shorter number — not with a seven-digit utility number. Two era flat-bottle designs were examined anyway: D48,007 (William Orem of Baltimore, for Mme. Isbell’s Toilet Manufacturing Co., 1915) — its drawing shows a tall, slender, tapering bottle that does not match; and D48,140 (Charles D. Wolfson, for the perfume house A. A. Vantine & Co., 1915) — a flat bottle by its figure set, but its drawing was not visually compared in this research, and its number could not in any case be embossed as “1079017.”

So what does the marking mean?

Documented practice (Glass Bottle Marks) is that a “PAT.” number on a bottle base usually references the closure, not the bottle — William Painter’s 1885 Baltimore Loop seal patent, for example, appears as “PAT 85” on the bases of countless beer and soda bottles. But that route is closed here too: 1,079,017 covers no closure. The explanations that remain are set out below, and the evidence cannot choose between them — each is labeled for what it is:

  • Most plausible (inference): one or more digits of the worn, raised, curved-base lettering have been misread — by us, from photographs, in glare. The true number would then be a different patent, not yet identified. A raking-light photograph or a pencil rubbing of the base, read digit by digit, is the one test that could settle it.
  • Possible, unevidenced: a mold-engraver’s error at the glassworks, or an erroneous marking carried on a reused mold. Both happened in the period; neither can be shown for this bottle.
  • Unresolved — the owner’s hypothesis: the string may not be a patent number at all. A maker’s style or mold designation — the glasshouse’s own number for the form — would sit on a base exactly like this, would reference no patent, and so would be invisible to every search in this section. It is recorded here as an open possibility: nothing found confirms it, and nothing found excludes it.
  • Established: whatever the explanation, the number as read does not identify the bottle, its maker, or its date — and collectors’ guidance is firm that a patent number is never a manufacture date in any case.

4 · The Date — Rebuilt from the Glass and the Finish

With the patent set aside, the dating rests where bottle archaeology says it should: on the glass recipe and the molded finish.

  • The faint lavender = manganese, lightly dosed or lightly sunned. Clear glass of this era was decolorized with manganese dioxide; ultraviolet light slowly turns it lavender (“sun-colored amethyst”). Manganese use “was commonly employed… until approximately 1915, at which time World War I cut off the main source (Germany),” and research summarized in Heritage Science (2024) puts the effective end of mass use at about 1917–1920, tapering into the early 1920s as selenium took over. Your bottle’s faint blush fits a late, low-manganese transitional batch as comfortably as an early one — and a bottle buried in soil is shielded from the sun that deepens the color.
  • The narrow neck + molded screw thread = machine era, weighted late. The transfer-bead collar is a machine’s grip mark; hand-finished mouths have none. The SHA’s dating research is specific: “very few narrowmouth bottles had continuous threads until the mid-1920s,” and toiletries pioneered the finish — Illinois Glass’s 1926 catalog praised its screw finish as one that “has gained great favor among manufacturers of hair tonics and toilet waters,” a finish absent from its 1920 catalog. Semi-automatic machines, which also molded threads, had made small-mouth bottles since the late 1890s and were fairly common by about 1912. And the narrowness itself deserves emphasis: a molded continuous thread on a neck this narrow, this early, is exactly the adoption the trade catalogs were remarking on — the narrow neck makes the early screw finish on this bottle the more notable, not the less.
  • The form was catalog-standard druggist ware from the 1880s into the 1920s and contributes the outer frame, not the fine date.

Made about 1915–1928 — most likely in the 1920s.

How this differs from the first edition: the window’s outer bounds stand, but the cleaned bottle’s narrow neck — which the first edition misread as relatively wide — shifts the center of probability later, and the faint tint no longer argues for an early, manganese-rich batch.

5 · The Maker — Unknown, and One Suspect Cleared

The bottle carries no maker’s mark, so no factory can be named. The class of makers who produced exactly this ware is documented (SHA catalog research): Whitall Tatum Co. of Millville, New Jersey (the era’s dominant druggist-ware house); Illinois Glass Co. of Alton, Illinois; Owens-machine licensees; T. C. Wheaton of Millville, whose early specialties included screw-cap vials for perfumers and pharmacists; and Hazel-Atlas Glass Co., headquartered in Wheeling, West Virginia, with container plants at Clarksburg and Grafton — the in-state possibility, unprovable without a mark. Bottles moved nationally by rail and catalog: findspot is not factory.

The Carr-Lowrey lead — investigated and refuted

One Baltimore name demanded checking. In Jammes v. Carr-Lowrey Glass Co., 132 F. 827 (1904), the Carr-Lowrey Glass Company — a Baltimore maker of pharmaceutical and perfume bottles — was sued for manufacturing a patented toilet-bottle design: Albert F. Jammes’s design patent D35,027 (1901). The court found Carr-Lowrey had taken Jammes’s drawings, made the bottle itself, and infringed; Jammes won. It is a wonderful story — and it is not this bottle’s story:

  • The Jammes design is a round, plain-bodied bottle with concave fluting at the neck and a star-ribbed base — not a flat-oval, ribbed-corner flask.
  • Carr-Lowrey’s documented bottle design patents run 1923–1939; its one screw-finish utility patent is 1931. Nothing of theirs sits in the 1,079,xxx range of 1913.
  • Its one documented ribbed patented bottle, the KI-1/KI-2 poison (c. 1914), is an irregular hexagon in cobalt or green whose base reads “C.L.G.CO.” over “PATENT APPLIED FOR” — words, and the maker’s initials. Your base carries a number and no C.L.G.CO. mark — the very mark Carr-Lowrey’s pre-1920 practice would lead one to expect.

Conclusion: Carr-Lowrey (founded 1889 in Baltimore’s Westport neighborhood by Samuel Carr and William Lowrey; closed in 2003 after 114 years) was a genuine perfume-bottle house in the right city and the right trade — and on the documented evidence, it did not make this bottle, and no connection to it was found.

6 · Production & Circulation

Whatever glasshouse blew it, your bottle was a product of the fully mechanized bottle industry — and the numbers of that industry are documented. The first commercial Owens automatic machines (1905) produced nearly 13,000 bottles a day with two men, against some 3,500 a day from a seven-man hand crew, and cut the cost of a gross of bottles from $1.80 to 10–12 cents (University of Toledo / Owens-Illinois exhibit; ASME). The later AR machine reached 50,400 bottles a day, and by 1923, 94% of all bottles made in the United States were machine-made. Stock druggist forms were sold by the gross — 144 at a time — out of catalogs like Illinois Glass’s 1906 edition, which devotes spread after spread to “Druggist,” “Extracts & Panels,” and “Extract & Toilet” ware and sells the paper labels to go on them; its 1901 catalog prices druggists’ packing bottles explicitly “Per Gross.” From the glasshouse, bottles moved by rail and jobber to drugstores and small makers nationwide. Honest limit: no production total for this specific form or mold is documented anywhere found — the industry figures describe the world it was made in, not a count of its twins.

7 · Purpose & Prevalence — How Widely Were These Used?

The flat-oval panel bottle was one of the default containers of the American drugstore age. The SHA’s toiletry research describes the class — perfume, cologne, lotions, hair products, Florida water — as smaller bottles, “rarely over about 10 or 12 ounces,” narrow-necked, thin-walled; the druggist shapes (French squares, Blakes, ovals) were “very frequently used by druggists for their products,” for prescriptions, patent medicines, and skin lotions alike. This bottle sits at the very small end of even that class range: at its owner-measured size — less than 4 inches tall, less than 3 inches wide, about 1 inch thick — its capacity computes to roughly 2–3 fluid ounces (§2). The scale of the trade that consumed them is a matter of record:

  • 61,865 drug stores operated in the United States in 1929 — 57,812 of them independent (a 1929 Drug Topics census) — every one a customer for stock bottles by the gross.
  • The national market in pharmaceutical products grew from 2,700 items in 1880 to 38,000 by 1916 and 60,000 by 1933 — the shelf-filling explosion these blank bottles served.
  • Toilet preparations were a national mail-order category by 1908, when the Sears, Roebuck catalog carried hair tonic to farm and town alike.

For this bottle, the contents remain an inference, and it is labeled one: the combination of flat label panels, dressed-up ribbed corners, a narrow neck, and an early screw cap best fits a toilet preparation — hand or skin lotion, hair tonic, or toilet water — the product class that adopted screw caps first; a druggist’s own liniment, tonic, or prescription preparation is the second possibility; a national patent-medicine brand is unlikely, because those were embossed. In-period color: a 1923 advertisement offered a trial bottle of Hind’s Honey and Almond Cream — a comparable screw-top toilet preparation — by mail for six cents. (The Hind’s bottles themselves are wider-necked and rectangular: relatives in product and price class, not form matches — the owner’s correction, and the reason the comparison here is of product, not of bottle.) These were everyday goods, priced for everyone.

8 · Survival — How Many Remain?

The honest answer first: no census, registry, or count of surviving bottles of this class exists — that negative was verified across the SHA’s resources, the collector forums, museum collection portals, price guides, and the marketplaces. Anyone who quotes you a survival number for unembossed druggist bottles is inventing it. What the evidence does support:

  • The class survives abundantly. The collector forums where diggers report their finds are blunt about it: unembossed bottles are dug by the hundred from single dumps (“I have dug 100 out of one dump”), and unembossed screw-top bottles “can be had by the thousands” in the woods behind any small town. Glass is nearly immortal in the ground; the discard stream of a 60,000-product drugstore age was enormous.
  • Museums hold them as assemblages, not counts. The Smithsonian’s patent-medicine collection preserves a whole Pennsylvania drugstore’s stock — 700-plus items — as a period document; individual druggist bottles sit in state collections as typology examples. No institution counts blank stock bottles by form.
  • This exact ribbed variant is simply not traded under a name. A sampling of current eBay, Etsy, and dealer listings found comparable unembossed amethyst bottles routinely — and no listing for a ribbed-corner flat-oval flask with a patent-number base. That is a negative search result, not a rarity claim: with no face embossing, the variant has no searchable name. What is genuinely scarce in this class is the paper label — the bottle survives; its identity usually does not. Yours is the normal case, preserved whole.

9 · What the Ground Wrote on It

The rainbow skin and staining are the bottle’s burial record, written by soil chemistry. Buried glass weathers by leaching: slightly acidic soil water draws the alkali out of the surface, leaving stacked, translucent, silica-rich layers a micron or less thick; light reflecting between those layers splits into iridescent colors — the same thin-film physics as an oil slick (Conservation Wiki/AIC; Corning Museum of Glass). The layers flake — your bottle’s peeling patches are delaminating lamellae — so the surface should keep the gentle handling it has had. Dissolved minerals precipitate as crust; sediment washes in through the mouth and cakes inside. One caution from the conservation literature: the old idea that iridescent layers accumulate one per year of burial “has since been discarded” (Brill 1961; Newton 1971) — the patina testifies to a long burial, never to a date. And a note on the purple: much of what the first photographs showed as deep color was this crust and staining; the cleaned glass keeps only its faint, true blush.

10 · West Virginia & Martinsburg — the Local Chapter

  • Glass country. By about 1910 the glass industry employed some 7,500 West Virginians — the state’s fourth-largest employer — and West Virginia accounted for an outsized share of the nation’s bottle production (West Virginia Encyclopedia). The raw material was local: high-purity Oriskany sandstone from Warm Springs Ridge in Morgan County, around Berkeley Springs, roughly 30 miles from your yard, was quarried for glass sand from the Civil War era and became one of the world’s largest melting-sand operations. The automatic bottle machine itself was invented by Michael Owens of Point Pleasant, West Virginia; his company’s Fairmont plant (1910) ran around the clock at up to 180,000 bottles a day, with Clarksburg, Charleston, and Huntington plants following.
  • But not made in Martinsburg — the honest negative. No source consulted documents a glass factory in Martinsburg or Berkeley County in 1900–1930; the county’s industry of the era was textiles, brick, limestone, orchards, and distilling. Like most of its kind, your bottle almost certainly arrived by rail — the B&O reached Martinsburg in 1842 — was filled or sold by a local druggist (period biographies record Arthur Merryman Gilbert as a Martinsburg druggist of over forty years), used up, and discarded on the lot.
  • The dry years. West Virginia’s own prohibition — the Yost Law, effective July 1, 1914, nearly six years before the nation’s — excepted liquor for “medicinal, pharmaceutical… purposes,” so tonics and toilet preparations kept flowing legally through drugstores, in bottles like this one, and kept being thrown away afterward.
  • Why it was in your yard. Before curbside trash collection, households buried refuse on their own lots — in privies, ash pits, and rear-lot trash pits. City archaeologists (Alexandria, Virginia) describe privies as “handy receptacles for the trash of daily life prior to regular garbage pickup,” often sealed when a family moved; National Park Service excavations of period privies recovered glass medicine bottles dated 1905–1920 — this bottle’s era and class. The National Register nomination for your own historic district records the rear outbuildings and service alleys of the Queen and King Street lots — precisely where such pits stood. Which exact feature yours slept in, only its dig context could say.

Martinsburg in a breath: laid out in the 1770s by General Adam Stephen along Tuscarora Creek — the creek this project is named for — and incorporated October 18, 1778; a B&O roundhouse town from 1842; its railroad destroyed in the Civil War (Jackson’s men hauled its locomotives away by horse team in 1861); and in 1877 the starting point of the Great Railroad Strike, America’s first nationwide industrial strike.

11 · Value

Candor, with the basis shown. Unembossed machine-era bottles sit at the common end of the bottle market: the diggers’ forums put realized values for the class at a few dollars; current asking prices for comparable unembossed amethyst utility bottles cluster at $15–$30 (asking, not sold); published guides place common early-1900s bottles at $5–$20, with richer sun-purpled, labeled examples reaching higher bands this faint, unlabeled bottle does not fit. Weighing all of it:

General estimate: $10–$25 as a retail or decorative piece; $5–$15 the most likely realized price in an online sale.

Two adjustments, honestly stated: the base patent embossing adds no documented market premium — no listing, guide, or forum prices a bottle higher for a patent number on its base; its value is research value, and this guide is the proof of it. And a buyer paying for the documented story — a photographed, researched artifact out of a National Register lot in downtown Martinsburg — might pay at or above the top of the range; that is a provenance premium with no comparable sale to pin a figure to, so none is claimed. The bottle’s worth, as with most of its class, is the history it carries — which is now, patent dead-end and all, unusually well documented.

Growth: The Money

Measured retail sales vs. counted users

Mainstream sales jumped; the user count held its line

Retail sales (SPINS)Past-year users (NSDUH)

SPINS tracks mainstream multi-outlet retail only — grocery, drug, and natural chains. Online and specialty-shop sales sit on top of these figures; industry estimates put the total U.S. market near $1 billion. Survey counts of users have stayed in a 1.6–2.1 million band across six NSDUH survey years while measured retail revenue climbed — growth is coming from wider distribution and higher-value products, not from a surge in users.

The Global Outlook

Every forecaster points up — they just disagree on the altitude

Global market size: estimates today, projection for 2032

Fortune Business Insights values the global market at $2.19B (2024) and $2.56B (2025), reaching $7.80B by 2032 — a 17.2% compound annual growth rate. North America is the largest region, and the U.S. is its engine.

Read forecasts with care: Market Research Future values the same market at $6.31B in 2024 on its way to $22.51B by 2035 — roughly 3× higher than the Fortune figures at both ends. The direction (strong double-digit growth) is consistent across analysts; the levels are estimates, not measurements, and this page labels them that way wherever they appear.

The Squeeze: Bans Catch Up

Eight quiet years — then five states closed in eighteen months

Two lanes, both moving: states closing vs. states regulating

Closed to legal sale (cumulative)KCPA-style rules (verified endpoints)

The ban count sat at six states from 2017 to 2025. Louisiana broke the stall in August 2025; Connecticut, Kansas, Tennessee, and Massachusetts followed in 2026, and North Dakota's sales stopped the same year — 11 states closed by the fall of 2026. Over the same span the regulated lane grew from the first four KCPA states (2019) to 24 states plus D.C. with consumer-protection rules on the books.

Feb 2014 — FDA Import Alert 54-15: kratom shipments can be detained at the border without examination. Still in force today.
2014 — Indiana and Wisconsin enact the first state bans.
Aug 2016 — DEA announces intent to emergency-schedule mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine in Schedule I.
Oct 2016 — DEA withdraws the plan after a flood of public comment — the market's defining reprieve.
2016–17 — Alabama, Arkansas, Vermont (2016) and Rhode Island (2017) ban kratom. The count stalls at six for eight years.
2019 — Utah passes the first Kratom Consumer Protection Act; Arizona, Georgia, and Nevada follow the same year. Regulation becomes the second lane.
Jul 29, 2025 — FDA recommends Schedule I for concentrated 7-OH products — explicitly not natural kratom leaf.
Aug 2025 — Louisiana bans kratom statewide (SB 154) — the first new state ban in eight years.
Feb 2026 — Connecticut places kratom and its derivatives in Schedule I by regulation (approved Feb 24).
Apr 1, 2026 — Rhode Island reverses its 2017 ban — the first reversal in the country — replacing it with 21+ KCPA-style rules.
Apr 2026 — Kansas bans kratom (two laws, April). Spokane, Washington's local sales ban takes effect Apr 8 — cities begin acting where states have not.
Jul 1, 2026 — Tennessee's ban takes effect (“Matthew Davenport's Law,” signed May 7).
Jul 6, 2026 — DEA publishes a notice of intent to temporarily schedule 7-OH above a set threshold.
Aug 2026 — Massachusetts places kratom in Schedule 1 by temporary emergency regulation (Aug 13).
Aug 26, 2026 — Three synthetic kratom relatives (MGPI, MGM-15, MGM-16) enter federal Schedule I temporarily. The leaf itself remains unscheduled.
Sep 2026 — North Dakota's licensing law passes — but no sale is lawful until the Attorney General's rules exist.
Oct 14, 2026 — DEA replaces its 7-OH notice with a broader one (7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above a new threshold). No scheduling order has issued yet.
Jul 1, 2027 — Kentucky's enacted sales ban takes effect — the next closure is already on the calendar.

The Pressure Gauge

Why regulators keep moving: the case counts

Poison-center exposures climbed with the market

The National Poison Data System logged 11,015 medically attended kratom exposures from 2015–2024. The earlier series tells the same story from the start of the decade: poison centers took 13 kratom calls in 2011 and 682 in 2017. A CDC analysis of the same system found exposures up roughly 1,200% from 2015 to record levels in 2025. Rising case counts — increasingly tied to concentrated 7-OH products rather than plain leaf — are the stated fuel for nearly every 2025–26 ban and for the federal 7-OH scheduling push.

The fault line is 7-OH: the FDA's July 2025 scheduling recommendation, the DEA's 2026 notices, Florida's emergency rule, Ohio's pharmacy rule, and Utah's 2026 law all draw the same line — concentrated and synthetic alkaloids on one side, natural leaf on the other. Which side of that line a product sits on increasingly decides whether it can be sold at all.

What It Means

The thesis, and the four reads behind it

The U.S. market for kratom is not shrinking in demand or revenue. Mainstream retail sales grew 21.9% in the last measured year, lifetime use stands at a survey record (1.9% of Americans 12+, more than 5 million people), and every major forecaster projects continued global growth. What is shrinking — quickly — is the territory where kratom can be sold freely.

1 · Growth changed shape. Revenue is outrunning user counts. The user base has been essentially flat at 1.6–2.1 million past-year users across six survey years, so the sales curve is being carried by mainstream distribution, extracts, shots, and other higher-value products — the same products now drawing the rules.

2 · The map narrowed in a hurry. After eight years without a single new state ban, five states closed in eighteen months, a sixth suspended sales, cities like Spokane began banning on their own, and Kentucky is already scheduled to close on Jul 1, 2027. Michigan's ban bill is still alive. None of that has yet dented the national sales line — the closed states hold only about 14% of the population.

3 · Regulation is becoming the operating system. The biggest markets in America — Texas, Florida, New York — are KCPA states. ≈44% of Americans now live under consumer-protection kratom laws, against 27% in no-rule states. For sellers, compliance (age gates, labeling, testing, 7-OH caps) is no longer a niche cost; it is the price of access to most of the market.

4 · The leaf and the extract are splitting into two markets. Federal action in 2025–26 targeted concentrated 7-OH and synthetic relatives while leaving natural leaf unscheduled, and Rhode Island showed a ban can be traded for rules. The industry's center of gravity is moving toward plain leaf sold under KCPA frameworks — and away from the unregulated potency race that built the pressure in the first place.

Melanotan I & Melanotan II

Two peptides, one borrowed name — and almost nothing else in common.

One became a licensed medicine. The other never did. This guide keeps them separate.

They share a name, a parent hormone, and almost nothing else. Melanotan I is a licensed medicine — afamelanotide, a prescription implant for one rare disease. Melanotan II is an unlicensed research chemical that has never been approved anywhere, for anything.

Both were engineered in the 1980s from α-MSH, the body's own tanning signal, because the natural hormone breaks down in minutes. Everything that separates them — effects, evidence, legality — flows from one design choice: Melanotan I was built to work mainly on the skin's pigment receptor. Melanotan II was built smaller and stronger, and it works on nearly all of them.

At the University of Arizona in the 1980s, biologist Mac Hadley and chemist Victor Hruby set out to build a longer-lasting version of α-MSH — hoping a drug-induced tan might protect fair-skinned people from skin cancer. They made hundreds of variants.

Melanotan I (then called NDP-MSH) came first, reported in 1980: the full hormone with two surgical changes that made it roughly a thousand times more potent in early bioassays and far harder for the body to break down. Melanotan II followed in 1989 — just the hormone's active core, closed into a ring.

Then their paths split. The first molecule was licensed to a company (Clinuvel) that spent decades running proper trials in light-sensitivity diseases. Melanotan II itself was never developed into a medicine — abandoned as a drug candidate, it was picked up by the internet.

The shared secret is the four-amino-acid core His-Phe-Arg-Trp — the sequence every melanocortin receptor recognises. Both molecules carry it. Melanotan I wraps it in the full hormone so it mostly speaks to one receptor; Melanotan II strips away everything that gave the hormone its selectivity, then locks the core into a ring that enzymes struggle to open. Smaller, tougher, and indiscriminate.

Five melanocortin receptors, five different jobs. A drug's personality is simply the list of receptors it can reach.

The FDA's own label puts Melanotan I's profile in one careful phrase: it binds predominantly to MC1R. At the licensed dose, its clinical picture is a pigment picture — darker eumelanin, more light tolerance — and nothing else is claimed for it.

Melanotan II is the pharmacology lab's reference non-selective agonist: potent at MC1R, MC3R, MC4R and MC5R in the same low-nanomolar range, with no useful selectivity window at any real-world dose. That is why one injection can tan the skin and cause an erection, kill appetite, trigger yawning and nausea, and nudge blood pressure — four receptor systems answering at once. Neither compound meaningfully activates MC2R, the adrenal cortisol receptor: whatever else they do, they are not steroid substitutes.

The teaching point

Selectivity is not a detail — it is the difference between a medicine and a hazard profile. Keep MC1R and you get a tan. Keep MC4R too and you get everything the brain rows control.

In practice, both compounds reach users the same way: a freeze-dried vial, bacteriostatic water, and an insulin syringe, injected under the skin. This section is the mechanics — what is documented, what is merely reported, and where the two part company.

Reconstitution, step by step

  • Swab the vial tops. Draw 2 mL of bacteriostatic water and inject it slowly down the inside wall of the powder vial — a direct jet can damage the peptide.
  • Swirl gently until fully dissolved and clear. Never shake. Cloudy or particle-filled solution is discarded.
  • The mix is now 5 mg per mL. Refrigerate immediately; the powder vial, before mixing, keeps in a cool dark place or freezer.

The injection itself

  • Subcutaneous: pinched skin of the abdomen or thigh, needle at 45–90°, sites rotated. New sterile needle every time; sharps into a proper container.
  • Evening dosing is the common pattern for Melanotan II — its nausea and flushing arrive within the first couple of hours.

Dosing schedules — documented vs reported

Read this box first

No injection schedule for either compound has ever been approved or validated. The only doses on a regulatory record are the implant's 16 mg every 2 months and the experimental regimens below. Everything under "reported" is community practice — real patterns, zero verification — and every nominal dose is only as true as the vial, which independent testing has found holding 43–88% of its label claim.

Melanotan II — documented: the controlled trials dosed by body weight at 0.025 mg/kg/day under the skin (about 1.75 mg for a 70 kg adult), on repeated days. Tanning appeared in 2 of 3 pilot subjects; nausea was dose-limiting and sometimes severe; erections were a trial endpoint, rigidity 38 vs 3 minutes against placebo.

Melanotan II — reported: a cautious first dose of 250 micrograms to test reaction; then 500 micrograms to 1 mg daily through a "loading" stretch of days to weeks until colour develops; then a "maintenance" of 0.5–1 mg one to three times a week, often timed before sun exposure. Reported, not endorsed — the gap between the reported maintenance dose and the trial dose is itself a finding: nobody actually knows the right number.

Melanotan I — documented: only the implant (16 mg, every 2 months, EPP). No injection schedule has a trial or a label behind it.

Melanotan I — reported: injected afamelanotide is used in the same daily pattern as Melanotan II, typically 0.5–1 mg — reported practice only, for a molecule whose studied, regulated form is a two-month implant.

The same molecule leads two lives. As Scenesse it is a licensed prescription implant for one rare disease. Everywhere else it circulates as a research peptide, dosed by injection — with no approved injection dose anywhere.

The licensed form — the implant

Scenesse is approved only for adults with erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP), whose skin reacts to sunlight with severe pain: EMA December 2014, FDA 8 October 2019, the first EPP treatment approved in the US. A clinician inserts a 16 mg rod under the skin above the hip every 2 months; it dissolves on its own. It is not approved as a tanning product, cosmetic, or skin-cancer preventive anywhere.

Commonest label side effects: implant-site reaction 21%, nausea 19%, fatigue 6%, hyperpigmentation 4%, dizziness 4%, darkening moles 4%. Because it darkens moles and freckles, the label requires a full-body skin examination twice a year. Severe allergy including anaphylaxis has been reported since marketing. Beyond EPP: vitiligo showed real promise in one 55-person trial (48.6% vs 33.3% repigmentation with light therapy) but remains investigational. No drug-interaction studies have ever been conducted — the label says so plainly.

The injected form — what is actually known

Injectable afamelanotide exists only as a research chemical: freeze-dried vials, reconstituted and self-injected like Melanotan II (see How They're Used, above). There is no regulator-set dose, no injection trial programme behind the vials, and no purity guarantee — the same product-quality problem documented for Melanotan II in the next section applies to grey-market vials generally. What the implant data do establish is the molecule's character at sustained exposure: pigment-first, with nausea the main systemic complaint and moles that need watching.

Melanotan II was never licensed, never put through a Phase 3 trial, and never given an approved dose. What is sold online — vials for self-injection, nasal sprays, drops — is an unregulated product wearing a research chemical's label.

Those tiny trials did prove the pharmacology is real: tanning, spontaneous erections (rigidity 38.0 vs 3.0 minutes against placebo in 1998), higher sexual desire, lost appetite, yawning and stretching. They also showed the cost — nausea was dose-limiting and sometimes severe. Claims beyond this — fat loss, physique change, safe cosmetic tanning, "loading schedules" by skin type — have no controlled human evidence behind them at all. There is no honest percentage table for illicit use, because there is no denominator: nobody counts the users.

What the case reports show

Single-patient reports cannot prove cause, and the melanoma link in particular is debated — users often tan heavily as well. Read these as a pattern of severe, biologically plausible events in a product with no safety database to set against them:

Changing & eruptive moles — and melanoma cases

Reports of melanomas, atypical moles erupting within days of injections, and moles darkening fast. Whatever the causal share, MT-II changes moles — which can mask the very changes melanoma detection depends on — and no user is getting the twice-yearly specialist skin checks the licensed cousin requires.

Priapism

Prolonged, painful erection needing emergency urology care. The trial effect, with no off-switch.

Rhabdomyolysis

Muscle breakdown with sympathomimetic toxicity after a self-injection (2012) — a kidney-threatening emergency.

Renal infarction

A kidney infarct attributed to MT-II in a 2020 case report and review.

PRES with seizures

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome — a brain emergency typically driven by a blood-pressure surge — reported in 2013.

Plus the everyday list

Severe nausea and vomiting, flushing, appetite loss, involuntary stretching and yawning, spontaneous erections — the effects regulators list when they warn against it.

The product itself is a second gamble

Australia's TGA found the same chaos in 2026: seized nasal sprays labelled 30 mg actually held anywhere from 22 to 54 mg. With an illicit vial or spray, the user multiplies two unknowns — what the drug does in them, and what is actually in the bottle.

“Unlicensed, unregulated, and potentially harmful.”
— BMJ, 2009, on melanotan use in the general population

Regulators, on the record

  • FDA (2007): warning letter — an unapproved new drug, marketed illegally; the seller, Melanocorp, later pleaded guilty to federal charges.
  • Denmark (2008, repeated since): an illegal medicinal product; protection claims "not documented"; stop use and see a doctor if concerned.
  • UK MHRA (2008 onward): an unlicensed medicine that may not be safe; supply and advertising are illegal (personal use itself is not the offence).
  • Norway & Ireland (2008–09): "dangerous and illegal tanning" warnings.
  • Australia TGA (ongoing): prescription-only with no approved product; supplying it in any form — spray, tablet, injection, cream — and advertising it are illegal.

Why the nasal spray can't dose honestly

  • No study exists. No nasal melanotan product has ever had its absorption measured in humans. The percentages on vendor sites have no published study behind them.
  • The nose fights back. Peptides are large and water-loving; the nose offers a small surface, clears material to the throat in about 15–20 minutes, and digests peptides with its own enzymes. Approved nasal peptides work because their dose was set for that route in trials — the opposite of an unlabelled spray.
  • The bottle is unknown. No set amount per spray, peptide degrading in solution, and lab findings of 22–54 mg in bottles all labelled 30 mg. Even a perfect nose can't deliver a known dose from an unknown bottle.

Injection risks

Non-sterile manufacture plus self-injection means bacterial contamination (a haemolytic Bacillus species has been isolated from illegal injectable peptides), abscesses, bloodstream infection, shared-equipment risks, and wrong doses from under- or over-strength vials — with no monitoring, no antidote and no recall system. For contrast: the licensed implant still causes site reactions in 21% of patients despite sterile product, trained inserters and 30 minutes of observation.

Interactions — the honest version

For Scenesse: no interaction studies have been conducted, full stop. For Melanotan II: none exist either. Warnings about combinations — blood-pressure drugs, erection drugs, appetite suppressants — are pharmacology reasoning about additive effects, not studied facts. Any source presenting a tidy interaction list for either compound is making it up.

If you have used Melanotan II

Regulators' advice, in plain terms: stop — don't finish the vial. Get a full skin check promptly, and treat these as emergencies: any new, changing or bleeding mole; an erection lasting over 4 hours; muscle pain with dark urine; severe headache, vision change or seizure; or passing much less urine. Take the product and its packaging with you.

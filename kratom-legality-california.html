California Kratom Law

No kratom statute at all — possession is not a crime, but state health officials treat every kratom product sold for consumption as adulterated and unlawful to sell, and the 2026 enforcement push has emptied most store shelves.

✓ Last verified November 22, 2026 — read against CDPH Food & Drug Branch guidance, Health & Safety Code §110555, and 2025–26 California bill records

The short answer

California is a legal state on paper — with no framework at all. There is no consumer protection act and no ban statute: kratom, mitragynine, and 7-hydroxymitragynine are not scheduled under the California Uniform Controlled Substances Act, and possessing kratom is not an offense. What California has instead is an enforcement position. The California Department of Public Health (CDPH), enforcing the Sherman Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Law, treats any kratom or 7-OH product sold or made for consumption as adulterated and unlawful — at any age — and since late 2025 it has run a statewide push of retailer letters, embargoes, seizures, and an Attorney General lawsuit that functions as a de facto commercial ban.

Local bans stack on top: San Diego and Oceanside were among the first cities in the nation to prohibit kratom, in 2016, and Los Angeles County and San Mateo County have followed in the current enforcement era. The result is the strangest posture of any large state — a product an adult may lawfully possess, that no store may lawfully sell for consumption, and that the Legislature has neither banned nor regulated despite repeated bills trying to do the second.

The Law Today

No kratom statute of its own — one health-code section does all the work

⚖️

California has no kratom-specific statute — nothing to read the way Oregon's seven sections can be read. The state's whole position runs through the Sherman Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Law, the state counterpart to the federal food and drug acts: because kratom is not an approved food additive, dietary supplement, or drug, CDPH treats every kratom or 7-OH product sold or made for consumption as adulterated under Health & Safety Code §110555, and therefore unlawful to sell or manufacture. Possession sits outside that machinery entirely.

the structural problem, in one line: in a KCPA state a seller can read the statute and comply; in a ban state a seller can read the statute and stay out. In California there is no kratom statute to comply with — only an agency's reading of a general adulteration law, applied to sellers while the product itself remains unscheduled and lawful to possess. Every fight in the timeline below is, at bottom, a fight over whether the Legislature will replace that posture with a law of its own.

What the enforcement position deliberately leaves untouched. CDPH's authority runs to products in commerce — manufacture, sale, and marketing for consumption. It does not schedule the plant, and it creates no possession offense. An adult in California who has kratom has broken no state law; the store that sold it to them, on CDPH's reading, has. That asymmetry — lawful to hold, unlawful to sell — is the defining fact of California kratom law, and it is why the state's status reads "legal" on most national trackers while its store shelves empty.

How California Got Here

Local bans first, regulation bills second, enforcement last — the interactive timeline

🗓️

California's path is the reverse of Oregon's: where Oregon legislated first and enforced lightly, California's cities legislated, its Legislature stalled, and its health department enforced anyway. Tap Details on any card for the full record.

🏙️
San Diego & Oceanside — among the first local bans in the U.S.2016

San Diego and Oceanside adopt some of the first local kratom bans in the country — a full decade before the state itself takes any position. San Diego's prohibition, written into its psychoactive-drug ordinance, covers both sale and possession.

Local bans adopted
These two cities were early movers nationally: at a time when most states had never legislated on kratom at all, San Diego and Oceanside wrote it into local law. San Diego's approach — treating kratom under a broader psychoactive-substances ordinance — remains one of the strictest local postures in the state, because it reaches possession and not just retail sale.
🌊
Newport Beach prohibits sale & distributionMarch 2024

Newport Beach prohibits kratom sale and distribution, adding an Orange County beach city to the local-ban map eight years after the San Diego County ordinances.

Local ban adopted
The Newport Beach prohibition is a sale-and-distribution ban: it targets the retail market rather than possession, the pattern most later California local rules follow. Its 2024 date matters — local bans were still accumulating in the years when Sacramento was considering, and shelving, statewide regulation bills.
🏛️
AB 2365 & AB 3029 — the regulation bills stall2024 session

Two Assembly bills that would have regulated kratom statewide — AB 2365 (Haney) and AB 3029 (Bains) — are held in Senate Appropriations, the committee where California bills quietly die without a floor vote.

Held in Senate Appropriations
Being "held in Appropriations" is the standard California mechanism for killing a bill on cost grounds without any legislator voting against it on the floor. The 2024 holds set the pattern the next cycle repeated: regulation bills can pass the Assembly, but the Senate has so far declined to move any of them to the Governor.
🌿
AB 1088 (Bains) passes the Assembly2025 session

AB 1088, the cycle's main regulation bill, passes the Assembly: a 21+ age floor, child-resistant packaging, a ban on child-attractive products, and 7-OH capped at 2% of total alkaloids. It is referred to Senate Health on June 11, 2025.

Passed Assembly — to Senate Health
AB 1088 is recognizably a consumer-protection act in the national KCPA family — age floor, packaging rules, an alkaloid cap — rather than a ban. Its committee analysis in the Assembly traces the now-familiar argument: a regulated market with product standards versus an unregulated one. The bill's Senate journey is the rest of this timeline.
⚠️
CDPH issues its statewide consumer warningOctober 24, 2025

The California Department of Public Health issues a statewide consumer warning: foods and supplements containing kratom or 7-OH are illegal to sell or manufacture in California. The enforcement era begins.

Statewide warning issued
The October 2025 warning (press release NR25-016) is the moment the state's posture becomes public and explicit. CDPH's theory is adulteration: kratom is not an approved food additive, dietary supplement, or drug, so any product containing it that is sold for consumption is adulterated under the Sherman Law — no kratom-specific statute required.
🏙️
Los Angeles County bans kratom & 7-OH salesNovember 2025

Los Angeles County — the largest county in the United States — bans kratom and 7-OH sales, and county inspectors begin retail checks. Local law now covers the state's biggest consumer market.

County ban + retail inspections
The Los Angeles County action pairs the prohibition with active inspection: county inspectors began visiting retailers to check compliance, rather than waiting for complaints. Coming weeks after the CDPH warning, it showed local governments moving in the same direction as the state agency — restriction by enforcement, ahead of any statute.
📜
CDPH letter to processed-food registrantsJanuary 9, 2026

CDPH writes directly to processed-food registrants: the sale and marketing of kratom and 7-OH for consumption is unlawful, citing Health & Safety Code §110555. The warning becomes a direct instruction to the regulated industry.

Sale declared unlawful — HSC §110555
The January 9, 2026 letter (sourced in full below) is the enforcement push's central document: addressed to the businesses CDPH already licenses and registers, it states the adulteration position as a compliance directive. For sellers, this letter — not any statute — is the operative law of California kratom today.
⚖️
SB 758 (Umberg) — Schedule I for isolated 7-OHJanuary 13, 2026

Senator Umberg's SB 758, which would add isolated 7-OH to Schedule I, is heard in Senate Health. The bill carves out an exception for 7-OH naturally occurring in the plant itself.

Heard in Senate Health
SB 758 is the ban-side counterpart to AB 1088's regulation: instead of standards for a legal market, it would schedule the isolated compound. The natural-occurrence exception is the bill's key drafting choice — it aims at concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products while leaving the plant's own chemistry outside Schedule I. Its progress is tracked in What to Watch, below.
🏷️
ABC warns its licenseesJanuary 15, 2026

The Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control warns its licensees — bars, restaurants, liquor and convenience stores — to remove kratom and 7-OH products, putting license discipline behind the health department's position.

Licensee warning issued
The ABC warning six days after the CDPH letter widened the push beyond health-code enforcement: for any business holding a liquor license, stocking kratom now risks the license itself. Alcohol licensees are among the most compliance-sensitive retailers in the state, and the warning is a major reason shelves emptied as fast as they did in early 2026.
📊
The compliance count — 95% and 3,300 productsMarch 2026

The Governor's office reports the push is working: 95% of businesses have complied, and more than 3,300 kratom and 7-OH products have been pulled from store shelves statewide.

3,300+ products pulled
The March 2026 figures are the administration's own scorecard for enforcement-without-a-statute: near-total retail compliance achieved through letters, embargoes, license pressure, and inspections. Whatever the Legislature eventually does, the retail market the bills were written to regulate has already been largely dismantled.
🗳️
San Mateo County votes 5–0 to ban salesApril 21, 2026

San Mateo County's Board of Supervisors votes 5–0 to ban the sale and distribution of kratom in unincorporated areas of the county, with violations punishable as a misdemeanor — the Bay Area joins the ban map.

Passed 5–0 — misdemeanor
San Mateo matters beyond its borders: a unanimous vote in a Bay Area county signals the local-ban movement spreading north from its Southern California base. Its misdemeanor penalty also makes it one of the stronger local rules — most California local bans rely on administrative enforcement rather than criminal exposure for sellers.
⚖️
CDPH & Attorney General Bonta sue a manufacturerMay 1, 2026

CDPH and Attorney General Bonta sue a kratom manufacturer, Krave, in San Diego County Superior Court. CDPH reports more than $5 million in product seized across the 2026 compliance push.

Litigation filed — $5M+ seized
The lawsuit escalates the push from retail compliance to manufacturer litigation, in the county where California's local bans began a decade earlier. The $5 million seizure figure, reported by CDPH alongside the filing, is the administration's headline measure of the campaign's scale. The case is pending — see What to Watch.
⏸️
AB 1088's Senate hearing is canceledJune 17, 2026

AB 1088's scheduled Senate hearing is canceled at the author's request. The regulation path — the only live bill that would have created a lawful, regulated California market — stalls.

Regulation path stalls
A hearing canceled "at the request of the author" usually means the votes were not there. With AB 1088 stalled, California ends the 2025–26 cycle where it began it: no ban statute, no consumer protection act, and an enforcement position doing the work of both. Whether the bill returns, and in what form, leads What to Watch.

Three Models, Side by Side

Ban states, KCPA states, and California's third way

🔀

⛔ The ban model — statute states

  • Mechanism: a statute schedules kratom or its alkaloids as controlled substances
  • Sale: prohibited statewide, by law
  • Possession: a criminal offense, not just a sale rule
  • Market: driven out of state or underground
  • Clarity: total — the law is on the books for anyone to read
  • Count: 11 states ban kratom outright on the tracker's current count

✅ The KCPA model — regulated legal market

  • Mechanism: a Kratom Consumer Protection Act legalizes and regulates the market
  • Sale: legal, under state product standards
  • Possession: lawful
  • Age: a statutory floor — 21+ in Oregon; Nevada (2019) and Arizona (2021) also regulate
  • Sellers: registration, testing, and labeling duties set by statute
  • Clarity: high — a seller can read the act and comply

🗂️ California's third way — no statute at all

  • Mechanism: no kratom law — an agency enforces general food-and-drug law against sellers
  • Sale: treated as unlawful — products are "adulterated" under HSC §110555
  • Possession: lawful — kratom is not scheduled
  • Age: no state floor; sale is opposed at any age
  • Sellers: no framework to comply with — enforcement is letters, embargoes, seizures, license pressure, and litigation
  • Clarity: the lowest of the three — the operative rule is an agency position, not a statute

California is the only large state running the third model, and the comparison shows its oddity: it reaches a ban-like retail outcome — emptied shelves, seized product, a sued manufacturer — without a ban, while leaving possession as lawful as it is in Oregon. Supporters of the approach call it using the law already on the books; the bills in the timeline are the Legislature's so-far-unsuccessful attempts to replace it with a model of its own.

Local Rules & Enforcement

Five named local bans on top of one statewide enforcement push

🏙️

San Diego (2016). Among the first local kratom bans in the nation: the city's psychoactive-drug ordinance reaches both sale and possession — still the broadest local posture in California. Oceanside (2016), its San Diego County neighbor, banned kratom the same year. Newport Beach (2024) prohibits sale and distribution. Los Angeles County (November 2025) banned kratom and 7-OH sales across the county and backed the ban with retail inspections. San Mateo County (April 2026) banned sale and distribution in unincorporated areas, punishable as a misdemeanor, on a unanimous 5–0 Board of Supervisors vote.

Also reported: local restrictions in Riverside County and Jurupa Valley, and in Anaheim a different instrument — a 2%-of-alkaloids limit on 7-OH rather than a full ban, the same cap AB 1088 proposed statewide. Local rules change faster than state law here; anyone selling or shipping into a California city should verify its municipal code directly, because the patchwork — not Sacramento — is where most California kratom prohibitions actually live.

Statewide enforcement. Above the patchwork sits the CDPH campaign: the October 2025 consumer warning, the January 9, 2026 letter to processed-food registrants declaring sale and marketing unlawful under Health & Safety Code §110555, embargoes and seizures of product (more than $5 million worth, by CDPH's report), the ABC's January 15, 2026 warning putting liquor licensees' licenses behind the policy, the Attorney General's May 2026 manufacturer lawsuit, and a CDPH consumer hotline taking reports of continued sales. By the Governor's office's March 2026 count, 95% of businesses had complied and more than 3,300 products had been pulled. No possession arrests are part of this picture — the campaign runs entirely against sellers, which is precisely what makes California's model distinct.

What to Watch

Two stalled bills, a live lawsuit, and a county-by-county spread

👀
  • AB 1088 — the regulation bill. The 2025–26 cycle's KCPA-style measure (21+, child-resistant packaging, no child-attractive products, 7-OH capped at 2% of total alkaloids) passed the Assembly and stalled in the Senate, its June 17, 2026 hearing canceled at the author's request. Whether it returns — in the next session, and with its coalition intact — decides whether California ever gets a lawful regulated market.
  • SB 758 — the 7-OH Schedule I bill. Senator Umberg's proposal to schedule isolated 7-OH, with an exception for 7-OH naturally occurring in the plant, was heard in Senate Health in January 2026. It is the ban-side vehicle to AB 1088's regulation, and the two bills' fates will show which model the Senate prefers — if either.
  • More county ordinances. San Mateo's unanimous April 2026 vote moved the local-ban map into the Bay Area. Watch for neighboring counties to take up similar measures; California's local patchwork has grown in every recent year and shows no sign of stopping while the state has no law of its own.
  • The CDPH / Attorney General litigation. The May 1, 2026 suit against the manufacturer Krave, in San Diego County Superior Court, is the enforcement model's first big court test. A ruling on CDPH's adulteration theory — upholding it or cutting it back — would do more to define California kratom law than any document since the January letter.
  • Federal 7-OH scheduling spillover. Federal movement on concentrated and synthetic 7-OH continues. California's bills already distinguish isolated 7-OH from the plant; a federal scheduling action would pressure Sacramento to align state law, and could revive one or both stalled bills.

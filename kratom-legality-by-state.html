⚖️ The Short Answer

Kratom is legal in most of the United States — but the map is splitting three ways. 11 states ban it outright, 20 states regulate it under Kratom Consumer Protection Acts and similar laws (age limits, labeling, testing, seller registration), and it is legal with no state framework in 19 states and D.C. The fastest-moving fight is not over the leaf at all — it is over 7-OH, the concentrated alkaloid sold in shots, gummies and tablets, which a growing list of states — and now the DEA — treat as a drug of its own. 🏛️ The Federal Picture
  • Kratom leaf and mitragynine are NOT federally scheduled. The DEA moved to schedule kratom in 2016 and withdrew the proposal after public and congressional pushback.
  • Since August 26, 2026, three lab-made kratom relatives — mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 and MGM-16 — are temporary Schedule I substances (through August 2028).
  • Concentrated 7-OH is next: the DEA's October 14, 2026 notice of intent (91 FR 65370) would place 7-OH above a threshold — more than 1 mg per gram, or a mitragynine-to-7-OH ratio under 100:1 — in Schedule I. The ruling can publish any day on or after November 13, 2026 and takes effect the day it appears. As of November 17, 2026 it had not yet appeared. Natural leaf below the threshold is expressly outside the action.
  • The FDA has never approved kratom for any use, treats it as an unlawful dietary ingredient and unsafe food additive, and detains imports under Import Alert 54-15 (last revised August 17, 2026). FDA and HHS formally recommended scheduling concentrated 7-OH on July 29, 2025.

📖 How to Read the Map
  • Banned — kratom or its alkaloids are scheduled / prohibited statewide.
  • Regulated — legal to sell under a consumer-protection law: age floor, labeling, testing and/or registration.
  • Legal — no statewide ban and no consumer-protection framework.
  • 7-OH rule — a separate law targets concentrated / synthetic 7-OH even where leaf is legal.

⚠️ Local Bans & Traps
  • Denver, Colorado bans kratom sales citywide even though Colorado regulates it statewide.
  • Sarasota County, Florida bans it inside an otherwise regulated state; San Diego, Oceanside and Newport Beach, California ban it locally too.
  • California trap: no state statute — but the state health department treats kratom sold for human consumption as unlawful, so most retailers will not ship there.
  • Spokane, Washington banned sales in March 2026; some Mississippi counties and New York counties (Rensselaer, Oneida) have local bans as well.
  • Travel rule: possession laws follow the state you are standing in — a legal-state purchase is still contraband across a banned-state line.

◆ Why 7-OH Is Treated Differently

An extract and a 7-OH product are not the same kind of thing. The difference is which alkaloids get concentrated — and the law keys on exactly that.

🌿 Full-Spectrum Extract

Concentrated leaf — the whole spectrum, together

Mitragynine — dominant

Schematic: in leaf — and in anything honestly extracted from it — 7-OH is a trace sliver of the alkaloid profile.

  • Made by extracting leaf, so every alkaloid concentrates together, in roughly the proportions the leaf grew with.
  • Mitragynine stays dominant and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) stays a trace — the same balance as the plant itself.
  • Regulators treat a true extract as concentrated leaf, because the ratio is the leaf's own.

◆ Refined 7-OH

One compound, isolated and amplified

7-OH — the main event

Schematic: refined products invert the leaf's ratio — proportions vary by product, the inversion does not.

  • Made by singling out 7-OH — usually by converting mitragynine into it — and pushing it far beyond anything leaf contains.
  • Because 7-OH is a much more potent opioid-receptor agonist than mitragynine, inverting the ratio changes the character of the product, not just its strength.
  • That inversion is what laws target when they regulate 7-OH as a substance of its own.
⚖️ The federal line

The DEA's pending 7-OH action draws the boundary by ratio: more than 1 mg of 7-OH per gram, or a mitragynine-to-7-OH ratio under 100:1, counts as a concentrated product rather than leaf. Natural leaf and true extracts sit far on the other side of that line.

That is why the map carries two kinds of marks: 8 jurisdictions in this guide flag 7-OH separately while leaf stays legal there. The legal split runs along the ratio — not the plant.

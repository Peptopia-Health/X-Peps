Oregon Kratom Law

Legal to buy, sell, and possess — under one of the lightest consumer-protection frameworks in the country: a 21-and-over age floor for buyers, and a registration desk for processors at the Department of Revenue.

✓ Last verified November 20, 2026 — read against ORS 475.392–475.404, OLIS bill records, and Department of Revenue rules

The short answer

Oregon is a regulated state — not a ban state, and not a free-for-all. Kratom is not a controlled substance in Oregon: neither the plant, mitragynine, nor 7-hydroxymitragynine appears in the state schedules, and possessing a kratom product is not an offense. What Oregon regulates is the sale. Under the Oregon Kratom Consumer Protection Act (ORS 475.392–475.404, enacted 2022), a retailer may not sell kratom to anyone under 21, and a processor — the wholesale seller — may not sell at all until it has registered with the Department of Revenue and certified that its products are third-party tested.

Oregon got here the long way: a pharmacy-board study bill in 2017, two failed consumer-protection bills in 2019 and 2020, a full Kratom Consumer Protection Act that passed both chambers in 2021 and was vetoed, and finally the stripped-down 2022 act that the same governor signed. The labeling rules, alkaloid caps, and synthetic bans from the vetoed bill are not Oregon law — a distinction most national trackers get wrong.

The Law Today

The Oregon Kratom Consumer Protection Act, section by section

⚖️

The whole of Oregon's kratom statute is seven short sections — ORS 475.392 through 475.404, enacted as chapter 41 of the 2022 Oregon Laws (HB 4010). It does three things: it sets an age floor, it creates a processor registration, and it hands the details to the Department of Revenue. Everything else commonly attributed to it — labels, alkaloid caps, product bans — was in the 2021 bill that never became law.

Status
Regulated — legal market under a consumer protection act
Controlling law
Oregon Kratom Consumer Protection Act, ORS 475.392–475.404 [2022 c.41]
Possession
Not an offense. Kratom, mitragynine, and 7-hydroxymitragynine are absent from Oregon's controlled-substance schedules (Board of Pharmacy, OAR 855-080-0021)
Minimum age
21 to purchase. A retailer who sells, distributes, or exposes a kratom product for sale to anyone under 21 is guilty of a Class C misdemeanor for each violation — punishable by up to 30 days' imprisonment, a $1,250 fine, or both (ORS 475.398)
Who must register
Processors — businesses that sell, distribute, or expose kratom products for sale on a wholesale basis to retailers. Selling before registering is prohibited (ORS 475.396). Registration is annual, through the Department of Revenue
Testing
To register, a processor must certify that all its kratom products are third-party tested to satisfy industry standards for adulteration (ORS 475.404). The Department's rule, OAR 150-475-6020, lets processors test batches of unfinished or finished product for contaminants
Labeling
No kratom-specific labeling requirement in the Act. Because the Act defines a kratom product as a food or dietary supplement, general food law applies on top — Oregon adopts the federal food and labeling regulations at OAR 603-025-0190
Alkaloid caps
None. No ceiling on 7-hydroxymitragynine, no ban on synthetic alkaloids, no alkaloid-content disclosure rule anywhere in Oregon law
Processor penalty
Civil penalty of not more than $500 for a first offense and $1,000 for a second or subsequent offense, appealable as a contested case under ORS chapter 183 (ORS 475.396)
Administrator
Oregon Department of Revenue — registration, fees (capped at the cost of administering the Act), testing standards, and penalties (ORS 475.400, 475.404)
Registration live since
July 1, 2023 — a processor may not sell into Oregon without a certificate of registration for sales on or after that date

“ORS 475.394 to 475.404 shall be known and may be cited as the Oregon Kratom Consumer Protection Act.”

— ORS 475.392, the Act's short-title section [2022 c.41 §1]

a drafting quirk worth knowing: every section of the Act carries the printed note that it “was enacted into law by the Legislative Assembly but was not added to or made a part of ORS chapter 475 or any series therein by legislative action” — standard Oregon practice for free-standing acts printed alongside the chapter. And the short-title section, 475.392, sits just outside the range it names (475.394 to 475.404). Neither quirk affects the Act's force: it is the law, and the Department of Revenue enforces it.

What the Act deliberately leaves out. The 2021 bill (below) would have prohibited adulterated and contaminated products outright, capped 7-hydroxymitragynine at 2% of the alkaloid fraction, banned synthetic alkaloids entirely, and imposed labeling with alkaloid content. The enacted 2022 act contains none of those product rules. Product safety in Oregon runs through one gate instead: a processor cannot legally sell unless it has certified third-party testing to the Department of Revenue. Retailers face the age law; consumers face nothing at all.

How Oregon Got Here

Nine years, five bills, one veto — the interactive timeline

🗓️

Oregon's path to regulation ran against scheduling from the start: the state's first kratom bill asked whether the plant should be banned, and every serious bill after it chose consumer protection instead. Tap Details on any card for the full record.

📜
SB 518 — the scheduling study2017 session

Filed at the request of the Senate Interim Committee on Judiciary, SB 518 would have directed the State Board of Pharmacy to study whether Mitragyna speciosa and its derivatives should be scheduled as controlled substances. It never left committee — and no Oregon bill since has proposed scheduling the plant itself.

Died in committeeOLIS record ↗
Context: SB 518 arrived months after the DEA's August 2016 notice of intent to place mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine in federal Schedule I — a notice the DEA withdrew that October after public backlash. Oregon's study bill was the state-level echo of that federal move, and it met the same fate: the Board of Pharmacy never scheduled kratom, and to this day Oregon's Schedule I rule (OAR 855-080-0021) does not list the plant or its alkaloids.
⚖️
SB 1005 — the first KCPA run2019 session

The Kratom Consumer Protection Act first moved as amendments to SB 1005: the package passed the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously in May 2019, with agency-regulation details negotiated with the Department of Agriculture — then the kratom provisions were stripped by amendment before the session ended.

Stripped by amendmentAKA legislative update ↗
The American Kratom Association's May 2019 legislative update reported SB 1005-A7 “passed unanimously out of the Senate Judiciary Committee with recommendations for clarifying how state agencies would regulate adulterated kratom products,” with the AKA's lobbyist negotiating directly with the Department of Agriculture. The bill went to Ways & Means, and in June 2019 the kratom language was amended out. Oregon would wait three more years for a KCPA.
🏛️
HB 4013-A — the short-session plan2020 session

Chief sponsors Reps. Post, Clem, and Barker proposed a two-step approach: ban purchases by anyone under 21 immediately, and direct the Health Authority, Liquor Control Commission, Department of Agriculture, and the Governor's Office to build a full regulatory plan with the industry by December 31, 2020. The House passed it; the bill was not enacted as the short session collapsed.

Not enactedLPRO 2020 Summary ↗
The Legislative Policy and Research Office's official 2020 Summary of Legislation records HB 4013-A under “Kratom Regulatory Authority” as Not Enacted, through House Economic Development and Senate Judiciary. The 2020 short session ended abruptly amid a partisan walkout, killing most pending bills; the kratom plan died with them — but its age-21 floor and agency-plan structure became the template for 2021.
🌿
HB 2646 — the full KCPA passes both chambers2021 session

The complete Kratom Consumer Protection Act: processor registration at the Department of Agriculture, labeling and testing standards, a 2% cap on 7-hydroxymitragynine, bans on adulterated products and synthetic alkaloids, a 21+ age floor, and a $1,099,977 appropriation to run the program. Sponsored by Reps. Post, Lively, and Smith DB with a bipartisan bench, it passed the House on June 24, 2021 and the Senate on June 26.

Passed — then vetoedEnrolled bill text ↗
HB 2646 was the national-model KCPA in full: registration before any sale; no adulterated or contaminated products; no product over 2% 7-OH in the alkaloid fraction; no synthetic mitragynine or 7-OH; civil penalties of $500/$1,000; and a first-year registration fee capped at $2,500, with the program to become operative July 1, 2022. It reached Governor Kate Brown's desk with broad bipartisan support — and stopped there.
✒️
The veto — Governor Brown says noAugust 6, 2021

Governor Kate Brown announced her intent on August 1 and vetoed HB 2646 on August 6, 2021. She backed the under-21 ban but objected to creating a regulatory program at the Department of Agriculture, arguing kratom oversight belonged to the federal FDA — and she named the shape of a bill she would sign.

VetoedLegiScan record ↗
From the Governor's statement: “The Governor supports efforts to prevent this product from reaching the hands of young Oregonians. However, the portion of this bill that creates a regulatory function at the Oregon Department of Agriculture is problematic… Governor Brown believes that the federal Food and Drug Administration is the appropriate regulatory body to oversee the importation and use of this product.” She added: “I would entertain further legislation to limit youth access without the state agency regulatory function included in this bill.” The 2022 legislature took her at her word.
✅
HB 4010 — the KCPA, stripped to fit the veto2022 session

Chief sponsors Reps. Stark, Lively, and Smith DB brought the KCPA back in the short 2022 session — rebuilt to the Governor's specifications. Registration moved from Agriculture to the Department of Revenue; the labeling rules, alkaloid cap, and product bans came out; the 21+ age floor, registration, testing certification, and penalties stayed. Governor Brown signed it in March 2022: Chapter 41, 2022 Oregon Laws.

Signed into lawOLIS record ↗
HB 4010 is the act in force today, codified at ORS 475.392–475.404. It took effect on the 91st day after the session adjourned, but its registration duty was phased: the Department of Revenue built the program through 2022–23, and the prohibition on unregistered sales applies to sales on or after July 1, 2023.
🧪
The rules go live — registration & batch testing2023

The Department of Revenue adopted the kratom rules — OAR 150-475-6010 to 150-475-6030 — on May 10, 2023, effective June 1, 2023. From July 1, 2023, no processor may sell, distribute, or expose kratom products for sale in Oregon without a certificate of registration, and registered processors certify third-party batch testing for contaminants.

In forceDept. of Revenue ↗
The Department's registration page states the operative rule plainly: “The legislation requires all processors to register with the Department of Revenue for sales occurring after July 1, 2023,” and registrants “must certify that all kratom products that the registrant will sell, distribute, or expose for sale are third-party tested to satisfy industry standards for adulteration.” OAR 150-475-6020 allows batch testing of unfinished or finished products — the testing backbone the statute itself only sketches.
🔍
The open question — synthetics & 7-OH2026

Oregon law still sets no cap on 7-hydroxymitragynine and bans no synthetic alkaloids — but the ground is moving. The DEA temporarily placed three synthetic kratom-derived compounds in federal Schedule I on August 26, 2026, and Oregon's own Schedule I incorporates the federal list only as of its April 1, 2025 edition, leaving the synthetics' Oregon status genuinely unsettled. In November 2026, county officials began organizing for a 2027 bill aimed at 7-OH.

Unsettled — watch 2027Statute & rules analysis ↗
Two instruments point in different directions: the Board of Pharmacy's Schedule I rule (OAR 855-080-0021) pins the federal list to the April 1, 2025 edition of 21 CFR 1308.11 — before the synthetics were added — while ORS 475.005(6) defines “controlled substance” by reference to the federal schedules more generally. Natural leaf, mitragynine, and 7-OH are unaffected either way: none is listed in any Oregon schedule. The concentrated-7-OH and synthetic products are the exposed flank, and they are exactly what the 2027 effort targets.

Three Models, Side by Side

What Oregon has, what it almost had, and what ban states do instead

🔀

✅ In force — Oregon's registration model (2022)

  • Sale: legal statewide; possession never an offense
  • Age: 21+ at retail — Class C misdemeanor to sell under-age
  • Sellers: processors register annually with the Dept. of Revenue
  • Testing: third-party testing certified at registration; batch testing by rule
  • Labels: no kratom-specific label law
  • Alkaloid caps: none — no 7-OH ceiling, no synthetics ban
  • Penalties: civil, $500 → $1,000 (processors)

📜 Vetoed — the full KCPA (HB 2646, 2021)

  • Sale: legal, registered processors only
  • Age: 21+ at retail — Class C misdemeanor
  • Sellers: registration at the Dept. of Agriculture
  • Testing: statutory testing standards + state lab program
  • Labels: mandatory labeling incl. alkaloid content
  • Alkaloid caps: 7-OH capped at 2% of alkaloid fraction; synthetic alkaloids banned; adulterated products banned
  • Cost: $1,099,977 appropriation; first-year fee cap $2,500

⛔ The ban model — Schedule I states

  • Sale: prohibited statewide
  • Possession: a criminal offense, not just a sale rule
  • Mechanism: mitragynine / 7-OH listed as controlled substances
  • Market: driven out of state or underground
  • Oregon's verdict: considered once — the 2017 study bill, SB 518 — and never seriously again
  • Count: 11 states ban kratom outright on the tracker's current count

Oregon is the clearest case in the country of a veto designing a law. Every difference between the first two columns above traces to Governor Brown's stated objection: no state-agency regulatory program. The 2022 act kept the consumer-facing rules (age floor, registration, testing certification, penalties) and deleted the product-facing ones (labels, caps, bans) — which is why Oregon's KCPA is simultaneously real and the lightest in the nation.

Local Rules & Enforcement

One reported city restriction, a quiet enforcement record

🏙️

Ontario, Oregon. Multiple national vendors refuse to ship kratom to Ontario, on the Idaho border, reporting a local ordinance restricting kratom sales there. That is the only Oregon locality that appears in vendor no-ship records; the ordinance text itself could not be verified against the city's published code for this addendum, so treat Ontario as vendor-reported — verify locally before selling or shipping there. No other Oregon city or county kratom ordinance appears in the sources reviewed.

Enforcement. The Act's enforcement is administrative, not criminal, on the supply side: the Department of Revenue collects registration fees and civil penalties (held in the Department's suspense account under ORS 475.400, with tax-law procedures applied by ORS 475.402), and an assessed processor can appeal as a contested case. The only crime the Act creates is the retailer's — selling to someone under 21. No contested-case penalty, prosecution, or appellate court decision under the Oregon KCPA has been reported in the sources reviewed for this addendum; the program's first three years have been quiet.

The scheduling backstop. Oregon's controlled-substance schedules are maintained by the Board of Pharmacy at OAR chapter 855, division 80. A full read of that division confirms what the statute implies: kratom, Mitragyna speciosa, mitragynine, and 7-hydroxymitragynine appear nowhere in it. Any future Oregon ban would have to arrive either by legislation or by Board rulemaking — neither is pending as of this writing.

What to Watch

The 2027 session is already taking shape

👀
  • A 2027 Oregon bill aimed at 7-OH. In November 2026, Marion County Commissioner Danielle Bethell warned county officials statewide about kratom, 7-OH, and nitrous oxide at the Association of Oregon Counties conference, and is working with State Representative Jules Walters on legislation for the 2027 session “to strengthen Oregon's response.” The gap she named is the real one: Oregon law limits sales to adults 21+ but “sets no limit on 7-OH potency.” Expect a bill that adds the product rules the 2021 veto stripped out.
  • The federal synthetics question. The DEA's August 26, 2026 temporary order placed three synthetic kratom-derived compounds (mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, MGM-16) in federal Schedule I, and federal movement on concentrated 7-OH continues. Whether those compounds are controlled in Oregon turns on the incorporation-by-reference question in the timeline above — a Board of Pharmacy rule update could settle it either way.
  • The advocacy landscape. The American Kratom Association backed Oregon's KCPA path from the 2019 committee fight through the 2022 signing, and Oregon remains a showcase state for the regulate-don't-ban model. Any 2027 bill will be fought on the same ground: age and registration rules are settled law here; potency caps and product bans are the contested frontier.
  • Registration enforcement. The Department of Revenue's program has run since July 2023 on certification and fees. The first contested penalty case — whenever it comes — will be the first real test of how the Act's light framework holds up.

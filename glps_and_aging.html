📰 Latest update — Nov 23, 2026
  • GLP-1s beat metformin in prediabetes — before diabetes starts: at the American Heart Association’s Scientific Sessions 2026 (Chicago, Nov 6–9), Mayo Clinic Arizona researchers presented a TriNetX analysis of 256,492 matched adults with prediabetes and obesity — 128,846 started on a GLP-1 (semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide or dulaglutide) vs 128,846 on metformin, none with diabetes or heart disease at baseline (Abstract MDP1524).
  • Blood sugar: progression to type 2 diabetes 25.1% on GLP-1 vs 37.0% on metformin (HR 0.69); reversion to normal blood sugar 37.3% vs 29.3% (HR 1.53).
  • Heart outcomes: fewer heart attacks (HR 0.84), ischemic strokes (HR 0.80), acute heart failure (HR 0.74) and cardiac arrests (HR 0.58), and death from any cause 41% lower (HR 0.59) over ~3 years of follow-up; ventricular tachycardia did not differ.
  • Honest caveat: observational records, not a randomized trial — association, not proven cause and effect; a conference abstract, not yet peer-reviewed; dose and adherence unknown; the four GLP-1 medicines pooled. A prospective trial is still needed.

🎯 The headline: two to three years younger?

The claim now making the rounds on business television: the world’s best-selling weight-loss drugs may also be longevity drugs. At the Aging Research & Drug Discovery (ARDD) conference, both Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk presented analyses from their clinical-trial programs indicating that GLP-1 medications slowed biological aging by roughly two to three years in the patients studied.

Geneticist Steve Horvath — inventor of the “Horvath clock,” the original epigenetic age test — called two years “a pretty strong effect” and said the data suggests GLP-1 drugs may be geroprotectors: medications that slow, or possibly even reverse, biological aging.

For scale: more than one in ten American adults now takes a GLP-1 for weight loss. If even part of the aging signal holds up, the implications go far beyond the scale.

Eli Lilly’s data told a similar story on a smaller scale. Using epigenetic clocks — which read chemical marks on DNA rather than blood proteins — Lilly’s tirzepatide analysis showed the clearest age reductions in the heart, brain, liver, and kidneys.

“All the clocks are telling a consistent story that we’re seeing a reduction in age.”— Kevin Duffin, VP of aging research, Eli Lilly, at ARDD 2026

Duffin added a note of proportion: this is a meaningful reduction, not decades — nobody is claiming thirty years. The effects varied widely depending on which clock was used and which organ was measured — up to four years on a heart-protein clock, less elsewhere — but every clock pointed the same direction.

🧬 How scientists measure biological age

You can’t ask a birth certificate how fast someone is aging. Researchers use biological-age clocks — algorithms that estimate how old your cells and organs act, versus how old you are.

🕰️ Epigenetic clocks (Horvath clocks)

Read DNA methylation marks — chemical tags on your genes that accumulate with age. Lilly used these for its tirzepatide analysis. The original Horvath clock (2013) kicked off the entire field.

🩸 Proteomic clocks

Read levels of key proteins in the blood to predict a person’s age and risk of dying. Novo Nordisk used these across its 10,000-patient analysis.

⏱️ DunedinPACE — the pace-of-aging clock

Instead of a single age number, measures the speed of aging. The UC San Diego team found semaglutide slowed that pace by 9% — in adults with HIV, a population that ages faster than average.

Why it matters: when independent clock types — DNA marks, blood proteins, pace-of-aging — all move the same direction, researchers take notice. That convergence is what made the ARDD presentations land.

🧠 The clock that matters most may be the brain’s (Nov 5, 2026). The Science special issue’s cognition Review (Börchers et al., Gothenburg / Copenhagen) weighs the same kind of convergence one organ over: animal studies show memory and learning gains, and patient-record analyses link GLP-1 use to lower dementia risk in metabolic populations. But the decisive randomized test — EVOKE / EVOKE+, 3,808 patients on oral semaglutide — shifted Alzheimer’s biomarkers without slowing the disease. A biomarker that moves is not yet a mind protected; the review’s authors call for trials that track cognition from the start of treatment, before degeneration sets in.

⚠️ The honest caveats

  • Company-presented data. Both datasets were presented by the drugmakers themselves at a conference — not yet peer-reviewed publications. Independent replication is pending.
  • The population studied. The analyses covered overweight or diabetic patients. Whether healthy people see the same anti-aging effects is unknown — that’s exactly what the new ARPA-H trial aims to find out.
  • Muscle loss is a real concern. In older adults, appetite suppression can cut protein intake and accelerate muscle loss — researchers flag this as the key risk to manage.
  • Side effects exist. GI effects are common; rare but serious risks have been reported. These drugs are not approved for anti-aging, and nothing here is a recommendation to take them for that purpose.
  • Clocks aren’t destiny. A younger proteomic age is a biomarker signal, not proof of extra years of life. Longevity has to be proven the slow way — with time.
  • Joints & bones (Oct 28). The 181-outcome study also found higher osteoarthritis (HR 1.48) and stress-fracture (HR 1.68) associations on GLP-1s — observational signals, not proof of cause.
  • The brain claim failed its big test (Nov 5). EVOKE / EVOKE+ — 3,808 patients, oral semaglutide, ~2 years — did not slow Alzheimer’s progression despite biomarker shifts. The lower dementia risk in patient records is observational and may reflect who gets prescribed these drugs, not the drugs themselves.
  • Eye warning on every GLP-1 label (Nov 16). NAION — a rare optic-nerve event that can cause sudden, painless vision loss in one eye — must now sit in Warnings and Precautions on the labels of ALL GLP-1 medicines, per the FDA’s Drug Safety Communication of November 9, 2026. The FDA pooled 126 trials (15 probable events; 6.4 vs 3.8 per 100,000 person-years) and a Sentinel cohort (HR 1.43 vs DPP-4 inhibitors; 1.19 vs SGLT-2 inhibitors, not significant), and noted cases reported for every marketed product in the class. Earlier estimates were semaglutide-only: 4.28× in the first single-clinic study down to a 1.32 rate ratio across 37.1M adults; the EMA’s June 2025 “very rare” conclusion (up to 1 in 10,000) was likewise semaglutide-specific. Causation unproven, no mechanism demonstrated. Sudden vision loss on these medicines means same-day eye care, and the medicine stops if NAION is confirmed.
  • Beating metformin — in records, not a trial (Nov 23). The AHA 2026 TriNetX analysis (256,492 matched adults with prediabetes and obesity) is observational: GLP-1 starters may differ from metformin starters in ways matching cannot capture, outcomes came from diagnosis codes, dose and adherence are unknown, and the finding is a conference abstract awaiting peer review. The 41% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.59) is larger than pharmacology alone would predict — a strong association a prospective trial must confirm, not proof of cause and effect.
  • Stopping means regaining (Nov 5). The Science issue underlines the standing limits: GI side effects, muscle loss, widely variable response — and weight that returns when treatment stops.

🔭 What’s next

🧪 UTMB Galveston — the first dedicated trial

University of Texas Medical Branch researchers are running one of the first US clinical trials testing a GLP-1 specifically for aging: adults 55–70 on tirzepatide for 24 weeks, then 12 weeks of follow-up, tracking epigenetic clocks plus physical function.

🏛️ ARPA-H — $38 million bet

The US Advanced Research Projects Agency for Health is funding a study of semaglutide in healthy adults over 60, measuring cognition, mobility, and sensory acuity — the outcomes that actually matter for aging well.

🎯 Retatrutide — the triple agonist

Nov 2, 2026: first phase 3 head-to-head win — beat Ozempic 2 mg on A1C (−2.2 vs −1.8) and weight (−20.9% vs −9.4%), TRANSCEND-T2D-2 topline. Investigational; next test is TRIUMPH-5 vs tirzepatide. Read the story.

🌍 Beyond GLP-1 — and who gets it

Nov 5, 2026: Science’s special issue marks the field’s turn to multi-receptor agents — and its Policy Forum (Blüher) presses the access question: global demand vs price and supply will decide who benefits. Read the story.

💊 The oral wave: orforglipron beat oral semaglutide head-to-head, showed modeled diabetes/CV risk cuts, and matched insulin on heart safety — as Biomea’s BMF-650 posted Phase 1 signals.

Fox News senior medical analyst Dr. Marc Siegel has also covered the UC San Diego findings on air, noting the cardiovascular and neurological angles alongside the digestive and thyroid side-effect profile — a sign this story is moving from conference halls to mainstream medicine’s radar.

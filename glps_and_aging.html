🎯 The headline: two to three years younger?

The claim now making the rounds on business television: the world’s best-selling weight-loss drugs may also be longevity drugs. At the Aging Research & Drug Discovery (ARDD) conference, both Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk presented analyses from their clinical-trial programs indicating that GLP-1 medications slowed biological aging by roughly two to three years in the patients studied.

Geneticist Steve Horvath — inventor of the “Horvath clock,” the original epigenetic age test — called two years “a pretty strong effect” and said the data suggests GLP-1 drugs may be geroprotectors: medications that slow, or possibly even reverse, biological aging.

For scale: more than one in ten American adults now takes a GLP-1 for weight loss. If even part of the aging signal holds up, the implications go far beyond the scale.

Eli Lilly’s data told a similar story on a smaller scale. Using epigenetic clocks — which read chemical marks on DNA rather than blood proteins — Lilly’s tirzepatide analysis showed the clearest age reductions in the heart, brain, liver, and kidneys.

“All the clocks are telling a consistent story that we’re seeing a reduction in age.”— Kevin Duffin, VP of aging research, Eli Lilly, at ARDD 2026

Duffin added a note of proportion: this is a meaningful reduction, not decades — nobody is claiming thirty years. The effects varied widely depending on which clock was used and which organ was measured — up to four years on a heart-protein clock, less elsewhere — but every clock pointed the same direction.

🧬 How scientists measure biological age

You can’t ask a birth certificate how fast someone is aging. Researchers use biological-age clocks — algorithms that estimate how old your cells and organs act, versus how old you are.

🕰️ Epigenetic clocks (Horvath clocks)

Read DNA methylation marks — chemical tags on your genes that accumulate with age. Lilly used these for its tirzepatide analysis. The original Horvath clock (2013) kicked off the entire field.

🩸 Proteomic clocks

Read levels of key proteins in the blood to predict a person’s age and risk of dying. Novo Nordisk used these across its 10,000-patient analysis.

⏱️ DunedinPACE — the pace-of-aging clock

Instead of a single age number, measures the speed of aging. The UC San Diego team found semaglutide slowed that pace by 9% — in adults with HIV, a population that ages faster than average.

Why it matters: when independent clock types — DNA marks, blood proteins, pace-of-aging — all move the same direction, researchers take notice. That convergence is what made the ARDD presentations land.

⚠️ The honest caveats

  • Company-presented data. Both datasets were presented by the drugmakers themselves at a conference — not yet peer-reviewed publications. Independent replication is pending.
  • The population studied. The analyses covered overweight or diabetic patients. Whether healthy people see the same anti-aging effects is unknown — that’s exactly what the new ARPA-H trial aims to find out.
  • Muscle loss is a real concern. In older adults, appetite suppression can cut protein intake and accelerate muscle loss — researchers flag this as the key risk to manage.
  • Side effects exist. GI effects are common; rare but serious risks have been reported. These drugs are not approved for anti-aging, and nothing here is a recommendation to take them for that purpose.
  • Clocks aren’t destiny. A younger proteomic age is a biomarker signal, not proof of extra years of life. Longevity has to be proven the slow way — with time.

🔭 What’s next

🧪 UTMB Galveston — the first dedicated trial

University of Texas Medical Branch researchers are running one of the first US clinical trials testing a GLP-1 specifically for aging: adults 55–70 on tirzepatide for 24 weeks, then 12 weeks of follow-up, tracking epigenetic clocks plus physical function.

🏛️ ARPA-H — $38 million bet

The US Advanced Research Projects Agency for Health is funding a study of semaglutide in healthy adults over 60, measuring cognition, mobility, and sensory acuity — the outcomes that actually matter for aging well.

Fox News senior medical analyst Dr. Marc Siegel has also covered the UC San Diego findings on air, noting the cardiovascular and neurological angles alongside the digestive and thyroid side-effect profile — a sign this story is moving from conference halls to mainstream medicine’s radar.

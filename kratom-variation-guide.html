🔬 Kratom Variation

Vein colors, strain names, vendors — the science of kratom inconsistency.

The short answer

Nothing in the chain from tree to bag is standardized, and five layers of variation stack and multiply.

The leaf is a 40+ alkaloid mixture whose recipe swings several-fold with season and soil.

Processing after harvest changes it again — including the powder's color.

Strain names and vein colors are unregulated commercial labels.

Vendors pool, blend, and store differently.

And your own dose, metabolism, stomach, and expectations rewrite the result one final time.

"Same strain, same vendor, different batch, different experience" isn't an anomaly — it's the expected output of the system.

Layer 1 — The Leaf Is a Mixture, Not a Molecule

A gram of kratom leaf is not a dose of a single drug. SCIENCE It is a variable blend of more than 40 alkaloids, and the ratios matter as much as the headline number:

AlkaloidWhat it doesTypical scale
MitragynineThe dominant alkaloid — a partial μ-opioid agonist with adrenergic/serotonergic activity. Your liver converts part of it to 7-OH.~0.3–2% of plain leaf; field extremes documented below
7-Hydroxymitragynine (7-OH)Far more potent μ-opioid agonist. In natural leaf it is only a trace — and largely forms through oxidation after harvest.0.01–0.04% in commercial leaf
PaynantheineSecond-tier alkaloid with serotonergic activity, associated with mood effects in the literature.Up to ~13% of extract fractions
Speciogynine / SpeciociliatineMitragynine's geometric twins (diastereomers) — serotonergic activity; speciociliatine also acts at μ-opioid receptors.Varies independently of mitragynine
CorynantheidineA functional antagonist at the μ-opioid receptor — it presses the brake while the others press the gas.Minor, but mechanistically decisive
MitraphyllineName-checked in marketing copy everywhere — yet in the University of Florida's 10-alkaloid analysis of commercial products it was below the limit of quantification in every sample.Negligible in products tested
Two powders can show identical mitragynine on a COA and still be pharmacologically different products — because a standard COA never measures the antagonist, the diastereomers, or the oxindoles. — Sharma et al., University of Florida

How wide is the potency spread, really?

In the largest field dataset — 745 samples collected in Thailand (Sengnon et al., 2023) — mitragynine in leaf from the same district ranged from 0.74% in October (rainy season) to 4.94% in June. Nearly seven-fold, before any human touches it.

A 3 g serving at 0.5% delivers ~15 mg mitragynine. At 3%, ~90 mg. Users experience that arithmetic as "this bag does nothing / this bag floored me" — and no strain name predicts which end they bought.

Leaf age compounds it: younger leaves measured 0.74% mitragynine vs. 0.40% in older leaves on the same plants (Leksungnoen 2025). Soil calcium and magnesium promoted alkaloid production. Insect-damaged leaves — alkaloids are the plant's defense chemicals — accumulated significantly more. Genetics matter too: researchers have identified multiple distinct chemotypes of the species, including one unusually rich in speciogynine instead of mitragynine.

Layer 2 — Processing Rewrites the Chemistry (and the Color)

University of Florida controlled research (Zhang et al., 2025) isolated what happens after harvest:

  • Withering leaves before drying raised mitragynine by 14–65% in one cultivar — from a step that is undocumented and unstandardized across the entire supply chain.
  • A 12-hour wither with low-temperature drying raised speciogynine 37–48% and paynantheine 35–67%.
  • High drying temperatures produced reddish-brown powder; low temperatures produced green powder. The color a buyer sees is substantially a drying outcome, not a leaf identity.
  • 7-OH appeared only at trace levels and only in certain seasons — evidence it is largely a post-harvest oxidation product, not a major constituent of the living leaf.

Vein Colors — What They Actually Mean

In the Thai field data, geography and season moved mitragynine content — but did not move vein color. And in native Southeast Asia, no color-strain distinction is made at all; the color-effect system is a Western retail construction. What each color word most plausibly reflects:

🔴 Red

Mature leaf, longer sun-drying and/or fermentation, more oxidation. The processing half is plausible (heat and sun redden powder; oxidation forms trace 7-OH) — but no study shows retail "red" consistently contains a distinct alkaloid profile.

🟢 Green

Mid-maturity leaf, moderate controlled drying. The middle of the process spectrum — and in practice the default state most leaf starts from before other colors are produced from it.

⚪ White

Younger leaf, indoor or low-light drying to keep a lighter color. Some vendors add ground stem/vein material to whites — a formulation step that changes alkaloid-per-gram in either direction.

🟡 Gold / Yellow

Not a recognized fresh-leaf vein type at all. Consistently an outcome — extended drying, partial fermentation, or blending of red/green/white powders — which is why yellow is the most vendor-variable category of all.

Bentuangie deserves its own line: it's a process sold as a strain — fresh red leaf sealed and fermented before drying. Fermentation plausibly shifts alkaloid ratios, but no peer-reviewed head-to-head against non-fermented leaf from the same harvest exists. A real process difference with unquantified consequences.

The Decisive Test — Names vs. Chemistry

One study measured both halves of the claim in the same vendor's products (Huisman et al., 2023). 644 users reported exactly the pattern marketing predicts — white highest for energy and concentration, red lowest. Layer 3 — Strain Names Are Product Codes, Not Botany

A retail strain name = a color word + a place word + marketing. There is no botanical registry, no cultivar standard, no legal definition. The geography doesn't survive the supply chain: SCIENCE Indonesia supplies over 90% of the world's kratom, concentrated in West Kalimantan. "Bali" is a historical trade label. Every "Thai" product sold before 2021 was almost certainly Indonesian leaf — kratom was illegal in Thailand from 1943 to 2020/21. Maeng Da is Thai slang for "pimp grade" — a grade claim some vendors pin on their strongest batch and others apply at random.

Treat every strain name as a vendor-internal product code: useful for reordering the same thing from the same vendor in the same season, carrying approximately zero information across vendors, years, or about where the leaf grew.

Layer 4 — Vendors: Recipes, Pools, and the Extract Problem

Blending is how strains are made

VENDOR DISCLOSURE One major vendor states its Super White Maeng Da formula openly: 70% white vein + 30% green vein, plus added mitragynine extract to reach ≥3% MIT. Legitimate, disclosed formulation — and proof that a name that sounds like a leaf type can denote a blend plus extract, while another vendor's identical name may be neither.

The extract end is a different product category

SCIENCE Products sold as "kratom extract" tested with mitragynine of only 2–6 mg/g but 7-OH at 22–75 mg/g — 5–28% above their own label claims — with profiles inconsistent with natural leaf (Brown et al., AOAC method). A 2026 analysis found capsule-to-capsule variation up to 48% within a single product. A University of Florida study found 85% of derived products (tablets, edibles, extracts — not plain leaf) mismatched their labels. Much of the internet's loudest "this vendor is fire / this vendor is bunk" discourse is this category confusion wearing leaf's clothing.

Storage and testing

Oxidation, UV, heat, and moisture degrade alkaloids mechanistically — but no peer-reviewed decay curve for bagged powder exists; vendor "<5% loss in 12 months" figures are untraceable. Indonesian leaf may spend weeks in containers and months in warehouses before bagging, so two vendors' "fresh" can differ by a season of storage. And COAs vary as much as products: most report only two alkaloids, methods conflate look-alike molecules, and a COA tests a sample — batch-specific, recent, from a named accredited lab with a full panel, or it's decoration.

Layer 4½ — Why Even a Good Vendor's Next Drop Differs

A new "batch" or numbered drop usually means a new incoming lot of Indonesian leaf — and everything above says it will differ: a different harvest month (worth up to ~7× in the field data), a different leaf-age mix (worth ~2×), different weather and pest stress, and artisanal post-harvest handling decided lot by lot — withering time alone is worth 14–65%.

The missing step: a pharmaceutical or mainstream supplement maker standardizes — assays incoming lots and blends to a target. Almost no kratom vendor does this for plain leaf. The COA describes the variance instead of removing it. Numbered drops make the variance visible and trackable — they don't reduce it. Drop culture is buyers rationally adapting: track the lot, not the name.
Can one lot number cover more than one color or strain? Yes. A lot number is whatever the vendor declares it to be — no standard defines it. One lot number can sit on a blend of several strains and colors, because a finished blend is a single lot. The same incoming bulk lot can be split and sold under several different strain names and vein colors. And one product lot can be built from several incoming bulk lots. A lot number identifies a vendor's finished batch — not a single harvest, color, or strain.

Layer 5 — You Are the Final Variable

  • Dose crossover. Low doses (roughly 1–5 g) skew stimulating; higher doses skew sedative/analgesic. A 2× stronger batch at your usual grams can flip the category entirely. In Smith et al.'s dose study, users' "too low" and "effective" thresholds averaged 2.64 g and 3.19 g — with standard deviations nearly as large as the means.
  • Metabolism. CYP3A4 — genetically variable — is the main enzyme converting mitragynine, and the only tested one producing 7-OH. Human half-life estimates run from ~3 hours (tea) to 23 ± 16 hours (whole leaf).
  • Stomach and preparation. Gastric acid destroys >20% of mitragynine within 20 minutes. A tea-bag infusion extracted only 0.062–0.131% mitragynine vs. 0.485–0.616% by full extraction of the same material. "Tea" is not one preparation, and its delivered dose spans a several-fold range by method alone.
  • Tolerance and expectancy. Tolerance moves the baseline weekly. And per the decisive test above, label-driven expectation co-authors every unblinded experience — including every video review where the strain name is known before the first sip.

COMMUNITY Johns Hopkins / NIDA researchers analyzing 370 Reddit threads (Rogers et al., 2024) found the folk model and the lab model agree on nearly every testable point. Their summary deserves the last word on this layer: "no two kratom batches are alike, nor are any two people." One poster's version: "It is a total crap shoot… eventually the vendor runs out and their new order from Indo isn't the same. They often just slap a name on the different batches and/or make all kinds of blends."

The Regulation Gap — Why Nothing Forces Consistency

There is no federal product standard for kratom: no required assay, no naming rule, no batch-equivalence requirement. The AKA's GMP program is voluntary and — in its own words — is "facility and process certification versus product certification"; the AKA "does not endorse the quality of the product." State KCPA laws are a patchwork (sources count 15–19 states) covering age, labeling, and adulterants — none requires lot n+1 to resemble lot n. The market's variance isn't a failure to enforce a standard. Mostly, there is no standard to enforce.

The Buyer's Rules for Consistency

  1. Buy the lot, not the name. The only unit with demonstrated consistency is a specific batch/drop from a specific vendor in a specific window. Reorder the lot number; treat its replacement as a new, untested product.
  2. Read COAs like an auditor, or ignore them. Batch-matched to the bag, recent, named accredited third-party lab, full alkaloid panel. Identical numbers across different products = red flag.
  3. Dose in milligrams of mitragynine, not grams of powder. Lot A at 1.5% and Lot B at 0.9% are not interchangeable at the same weight — B carries ~40% less primary alkaloid per gram.
  4. Use the natural fingerprint. Plain leaf: mitragynine dominant, 7-OH at trace 0.01–0.04%. A COA showing 7-OH near or above ~0.1% of the powder is not behaving like plain leaf.
  5. Discount color and origin claims to zero. Reward specific process disclosure instead — harvest month, drying method, fermentation time, lot number, full-panel COA.
  6. Treat vendor reputation as perishable. It describes recent lots and the current supply chain, not a durable trait. One weak drop is evidence about a lot before it's evidence about a vendor.
  7. Control your own variables first. Weigh it. Same preparation, same stomach state, same timing. Spoon-and-eyeball measurement manufactures "batch differences" out of thin air.
  8. Sample every new lot. Small test quantities per lot, notes keyed to lot numbers, larger buys only within a verified lot.
  9. Prefer fast turnover; store like a dried botanical. Sealed, cool, dark, dry. Be skeptical of deep-discount old stock — while knowing a faded bag may be a weak harvest, not a storage casualty.
  10. Know what the badges mean. AKA GMP = audited process and labeling discipline, explicitly not a potency or consistency endorsement. A state KCPA = that state's age/labeling rules, not batch equivalence.

The HPT Axis — How TSH Works

A Thermostat, a Furnace, and a Brake Pedal

Lead-in: TSH — thyroid-stimulating hormone, also called thyrotropin — is made by the anterior pituitary gland. It is not a thyroid hormone at all. It is the pituitary's instruction to the thyroid: "make more hormone." The American Thyroid Association calls TSH the "early warning system" of thyroid function, because it moves first — and moves the most — when the thyroid begins to fail or to overrun.

The system it belongs to is the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis, a classic three-step chain with a feedback brake:

  • Step 1 — Hypothalamus releases TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone).
  • Step 2 — Anterior pituitary responds to TRH by releasing TSH.
  • Step 3 — Thyroid gland responds to TSH by releasing mostly T4 (thyroxine, the storage form) and some T3 (triiodothyronine, the active form). Tissues convert T4 into T3 as needed; T3 is roughly 3–4 times as potent as T4, and the brain carries a high density of T3 receptors — which is why this axis is a mental-health axis, not just a metabolic one.
  • The brake: as T4/T3 levels rise, they feed back on the pituitary and hypothalamus and turn TSH down. As they fall, the brake releases and TSH climbs.

The single most useful fact in this guide follows from that brake: TSH moves in the opposite direction to thyroid hormone, and it moves logarithmically — a small fall in free T4 produces a large rise in TSH. That is why a TSH of 15 is not "a little high." The pituitary is shouting.

The HPT axis. Down the middle: stimulation. Up the left: the feedback brake. When the furnace (thyroid) under-produces, the thermostat (pituitary) calls louder — TSH rises. When the furnace over-produces, TSH is silenced — it falls, often to undetectable.

The two directions, memorized once

TSH HIGH = thyroid LOW (usually)

The gland is failing (primary hypothyroidism), so the pituitary pushes harder. The higher the TSH, the harder it is pushing. The exception that proves the rule: if the pituitary itself fails, TSH stays low or normal while T4 falls — central (secondary) hypothyroidism. That pattern — low free T4 without a raised TSH — is rare but must never be missed, because TSH alone will not find it.

TSH LOW = thyroid HIGH (usually)

The gland is over-producing (Graves' disease, toxic nodules, thyroiditis leak, or too much levothyroxine), so the pituitary goes quiet. The same central exception applies in reverse: a pituitary TSH-secreting tumor is a rare cause of high hormone with a non-suppressed TSH.

Nursing translation: on a psychiatric unit, TSH is a brain lab. Thyroid hormone tunes serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine signaling, cerebral metabolism, and sleep architecture. When this axis drifts, the first organ to complain is often the one your unit treats.

The Tests — What Each One Answers

TSH First · Reflex Free T4 · Antibodies When It Matters

Lead-in: the ATA's starting point is simple: TSH is the single best first test of thyroid function in a patient whose pituitary is intact. Most modern order sets use TSH with reflex free T4 — the lab runs TSH first and automatically adds a free T4 when TSH falls outside range. Everything else in the panel answers a specific second question.

🕗 Draw notes that change the answer

  • Morning, and the same time every time. TSH is pulsatile with a circadian rhythm — it peaks overnight and in the early morning and falls through the day. Serial monitoring values are only comparable when drawn at a consistent time; morning draws are the convention.
  • Biotin — the fake Graves' panel. High-dose biotin (hair/skin/nail supplements, 5,000–10,000 mcg) hijacks the biotin–streptavidin chemistry most immunoassays use: sandwich assays (TSH) read falsely low while competitive assays (free T4, free T3) read falsely high — a perfect forgery of Graves' disease. The FDA flagged this in a 2017 safety communication (updated 2019). The ATA's rule: hold biotin for at least 2 days before the draw — and ask every "hyperthyroid" patient what supplements they take before anyone treats the number.
  • Heparin. Even small heparin doses (a 5,000-unit subcutaneous prophylactic dose is enough) trigger in-vitro generation of free fatty acids in the sample tube, which displace T4 from its binding proteins and falsely elevate measured free T4. Draw thyroid labs before the heparin dose, or flag the timing to the lab.
  • Acute non-thyroidal illness (NTIS). Severe illness of any kind rewrites the panel without any thyroid disease: T3 falls first, then T4 in sicker patients, TSH runs low-normal — and during recovery, TSH can spike transiently above 20 before settling. An acutely medically ill patient's TFTs are a rumor, not a verdict: repeat them when the patient is well. (On a psych unit this cuts both ways — an acutely psychotic, sleepless, not-eating patient is physiologically stressed; a wildly abnormal panel still gets worked up, but a borderline one gets repeated.)
  • Levothyroxine timing. For patients already on levothyroxine, draw before the morning dose — a post-dose sample shows a transient free T4 bump that muddies titration.

Pregnancy — the range moves down

Pregnancy shifts the whole axis: hCG stimulates the TSH receptor in the first trimester and TSH falls. The ATA's 2017 pregnancy guideline asks labs to use population- and trimester-specific ranges; when those are not available, the practical default is an upper TSH limit of about 4.0 mIU/L in the first trimester, versus 4.5 outside pregnancy. Approximate trimester ranges consistent with that guideline:

TrimesterApproximate TSH range (mIU/L)Note
First0.1 – 4.0Lowest point of pregnancy; hCG effect peaks here.
Second0.2 – 4.0Climbing back toward the non-pregnant range.
Third0.3 – 4.0Near baseline by delivery.

Older fixed cutoffs — 2.5 in the first trimester, 3.0 in the second and third — are still printed in many references and lab systems; know both, and follow your lab's pregnancy-specific range when it exists. On levothyroxine before pregnancy, the dose typically needs to rise early in pregnancy with TSH checked about every 4 weeks until mid-pregnancy (ATA 2017) — flag any pregnant patient on thyroid medication for prompt endocrine/OB coordination.

Age — the range moves up

TSH drifts upward with normal aging. Age-specific data put the upper limit near 5.8 mIU/L at ages 60–79 and 6.7 mIU/L at 80+ — so a TSH of 5.2 in a well 84-year-old may be that patient's normal, while the same value in a depressed 30-year-old deserves a second look. This is one reason guidelines are cautious about treating mild elevations in the very old (see the TRUST trial, next sections).

From Result to Meaning — The Testing Algorithm

Read TSH and Free T4 Together — Always

Lead-in: no TSH value is interpreted alone. The pattern of TSH + free T4 together sorts nearly every patient into one of six boxes, and each box has a different psychiatric meaning and a different next step.

The working algorithm. TSH sorts the direction; free T4 sets the severity; antibodies name the cause; the three artifact questions protect you from treating a lab error.

Repeat before you label

A single mildly elevated TSH is not a diagnosis. Roughly a quarter of mildly elevated values normalize on a repeat draw in 6–8 weeks — early disease fluctuates, antibodies take time to declare themselves, and transient pushes (illness recovery, a night-shift draw, lab variation) are common. The standard pattern for a first mild elevation (4.5–10) with normal free T4 is: repeat TSH + free T4 in 6–8 weeks, add anti-TPO once, and treat the trend — not the single number.

The Deep Dive — TSH Level by Level

What a Rising TSH Does to the Mind

Lead-in: hypothyroidism is called "the great imitator" on psychiatric units for a reason: its earliest and loudest symptoms are psychiatric — low mood, apathy, slowed thinking, poor concentration — arriving months before the classic cold, dry, puffy physical picture completes itself. Depression is present in a substantial share of hypothyroid patients in clinical series, and hypothyroidism remains one of the most common reversible causes of a depressive presentation. Read the bands below as a dimmer switch being turned down: the brain slows in proportion to how hard the pituitary is shouting.

The severity spectrum. The flag sits at 15 — inside the 10–20 band, on the far side of every major guideline's treatment threshold.

Band 1 — TSH 4.5–10: subclinical hypothyroidism

Definition: TSH above range with a normal free T4 — the gland is compensating, at a cost. It is common (roughly 3–8% of adults, more often women and older patients) and it is a risk state, not yet a hormone deficiency.

The mind at 4.5–10: many patients feel nothing. A subset report exactly the complaints that fill a psych unit's intake forms — fatigue, low mood, mental fog, weight creeping up. The honest evidence picture: the association with depressive symptoms at this band is real but modest and inconsistent, and the large TRUST trial (levothyroxine vs placebo in adults 65+ with subclinical hypothyroidism) found no improvement in symptoms or tiredness from treating TSH under 10. A 2019 BMJ rapid recommendation likewise advises against routine treatment of subclinical disease — with named exceptions (pregnancy or planning it, TSH over 20, very young patients, severe symptoms).

Nursing posture: do not let a 6.8 be charted as "the cause" of a depression — and do not let it be ignored either. The job at this band is surveillance: repeat in 6–8 weeks, anti-TPO once (positive antibodies raise the odds of progression), and document the trend for the prescriber.

⭐ Band 2 — TSH 15: the number this guide is about

What 15 usually means. Fifteen is roughly three times the top of the normal range — the pituitary straining against a gland that is losing. Two patients can both read 15 and be in different diseases, and free T4 is what separates them:

  • 15 with a normal free T4 = severe subclinical hypothyroidism. Hormone levels are still defended — but the defense is expensive and failing.
  • 15 with a low free T4 = overt hypothyroidism. The gland has lost; circulating hormone is genuinely low; symptoms are expected, not optional.

Why >10 is the line. The AACE/ATA hypothyroidism guideline (Garber et al., 2012) recommends considering levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism once TSH exceeds 10 mIU/L (Grade B). The reasoning is outcomes, not cosmetics: pooled cohort data (Rodondi et al., JAMA 2010) show coronary heart disease events climbing with TSH — a hazard ratio of about 1.89 at TSH 10–19.9 — and progression to overt failure becomes the rule rather than the exception. Below 10, treatment is a judgment call; above 10, watchful waiting needs a reason. At 15, that reasoning applies with margin to spare — and it applies whether or not the free T4 has fallen yet.

The mind at 15. This is the band where the psychiatric picture stops being subtle:

  • Depression — low mood, anhedonia, hypersomnia, psychomotor slowing; frequently the presenting complaint. Antidepressant response is blunted while the thyroid is untreated.
  • Apathy that mimics negative symptoms — flat, slow, indifferent; easily misread as schizophrenia's negative syndrome or as "unmotivated" in a personality-disorder frame.
  • Cognitive slowing — bradyphrenia, poor concentration, forgetfulness; in older adults a convincing pseudodementia that reverses with hormone replacement, unlike true neurodegeneration.
  • The paradox: a minority present wired rather than slowed — irritable, anxious, restless, sleeping poorly. Hypothyroidism does not always look slow, especially early or while it is developing; do not rule the axis out because the patient is agitated.

Nursing posture at 15: this value gets same-day prescriber notification, a free T4 confirmation if it is not already paired, an anti-TPO if never drawn, and a note in the treatment plan that psychiatric symptoms may partly be thyroid symptoms — because the antidepressant plan, the expected-length-of-stay conversation, and the "treatment-resistant" label all change if they are.

Band 3 — TSH 25: overt failure, compounding symptoms

By the mid-20s, free T4 is low in most patients — this is overt hypothyroidism until proven otherwise. The physical exam starts agreeing with the lab: bradycardia, cool dry skin, slowed reflex relaxation, weight gain, constipation, cold intolerance. Mentally: deeper depression, marked cognitive slowing, sometimes frank confusion. Patients at this level have often been accumulating "noncompliance" and "won't engage" notes for months; what they have is a failing gland and a brain running on low hormone. Treatment is not optional, and full replacement dosing (weight-based) is usually appropriate in younger patients without cardiac disease.

Band 4 — TSH 50: severe, long-standing disease

Fifties and above mean months to years of untreated failure. Expect the full myxedematous picture: periorbital and peripheral non-pitting edema, coarse features, hoarse voice, hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation. The psychiatric risk at this depth escalates from depression to psychosis — the classic "myxedema madness" described by Richard Asher in 1949 (14 cases; 9 recovered dramatically on thyroid hormone alone). Across the literature, roughly 5–15% of myxedematous patients develop psychosis — paranoid, disorganized, or hallucinating presentations that can be indistinguishable from a primary psychotic disorder until the lab comes back. Every first-episode psychosis workup earns its TSH right here.

Band 5 — TSH 100+: the edge of myxedema coma

Triple-digit TSH values — 100, 200, occasionally higher — mark profound, prolonged failure. The patient is one stressor away from myxedema coma (Section: The Emergency Bookends): a cold, sedative dose, an infection, a stroke, or simple nonadherence can tip severe hypothyroidism into a life-threatening decompensation with reported mortality of 30–60%. Nursing care at this band is medical-watch care: temperature, respiratory rate and depth, level of consciousness, and extreme caution with sedating medications — the myxedematous brain and body clear sedatives and opioids dangerously slowly.

A ceiling note: TSH does not rise forever. Because pituitary output saturates and assays cap out, the number's job at the top end is not precision — it is triage. 60 and 160 both mean the same thing to the nurse: severe, treat now, watch closely.

The Mirror Image — Suppressed TSH

Hyperthyroidism: the Brain in Overdrive

Lead-in: flip the axis and the psychiatry flips with it. A suppressed TSH (< 0.4, often < 0.01) with high free T4/T3 — Graves' disease, toxic nodular goiter, a thyroiditis leak, or excess levothyroxine — produces a hyperadrenergic, hypermetabolic brain: anxiety, panic, insomnia, tremor, pressured energy. Anxiety disorders are strikingly common in hyperthyroid patients in clinical series, and the syndrome can escalate to mania and psychosis — hyperthyroid psychosis is a recognized first-break workup item, especially with a racing pulse that no PRN seems to slow.

  • The classic tells: resting tachycardia that persists in sleep, fine tremor, heat intolerance and sweating in a cool room, weight loss despite a good appetite, lid lag, frequent stools, new atrial fibrillation in an older patient.
  • The subclinical version matters too: TSH suppressed with normal free T4/T3 still carries cardiac risk — in adults over 60, a TSH below 0.3 is associated with roughly triple the risk of atrial fibrillation.
  • The iatrogenic trap: a psychiatric patient "failing" levothyroxine may simply be over-replaced. Always check what dose the TFTs belong to before labeling anxiety as a new disorder.

Same six bedside observations, opposite directions. Heart rate low/slow vs high/racing · energy flat vs wired · sleep long vs broken · appetite down vs ravenous · speech slowed vs pressured · temperature cold-intolerant vs heat-intolerant.

The bedside differential in one breath

Hypo: bradycardia · hypothermia · weight gain · constipation · dry cool skin · delayed reflexes · slowed speech · hypersomnia. Hyper: tachycardia/AF · heat intolerance · weight loss · diarrhea · warm moist skin · brisk reflexes · pressured speech · insomnia. When a "manic" patient has a resting pulse of 128, a tremor you can see across the room, and has lost 20 pounds eating normally, the differential starts with the thyroid — and the TSH is already in the chart.

Bipolar Disorder, Lithium & the Thyroid

The Axis Inside the Mood Chart

Rapid cycling and the low thyroid

Lead-in: few links in psychiatry are older or more practical. In the classic study of 30 rapid-cycling bipolar patients (Bauer & Whybrow's line of research, Arch Gen Psychiatry 1990), 23% had grade I (overt) hypothyroidism, with further patients in the milder grades; broader clinical reviews put thyroid abnormalities in roughly 20–30% of rapid cyclers, and some reviews report subclinical hypothyroidism in a substantially higher share. The association's strength is debated — at least one matched study found no difference in prevalence — but the clinical rule that survives every version of the argument is the same: a TSH belongs in the workup of every bipolar patient, and it is mandatory in rapid cycling, because untreated hypothyroidism can drive cycling that no mood stabilizer will settle.

Lithium-induced hypothyroidism

Lithium concentrates in the thyroid and inhibits hormone release; the gland compensates until, in a sizable minority, it cannot. Real-world data: in a large Keele University cohort of lithium patients, 19.9% developed an abnormal TSH by 3 years, and 91.4% of thyroid dysfunction appeared within the first 3 years of therapy. Psychiatry teaching commonly summarizes the risk as up to about 1 in 5 lithium patients. Women and patients with pre-existing thyroid antibodies carry the highest risk. Lithium also unmasks or worsens autoimmunity, and rarely does the opposite — hyperthyroidism is described but uncommon.

The crucial management point: lithium-induced hypothyroidism is not a reason to stop lithium. The standard move is to keep the mood stabilizer and add levothyroxine — treat the thyroid, protect the mood. Nurses who frame it that way ("we add a thyroid tablet; we don't take away the medicine that keeps you stable") prevent a common adherence panic.

The lithium thyroid monitoring schedule

  • Baseline TFTs (TSH ± free T4, antibodies if indicated) before lithium starts — alongside renal function and calcium.
  • Every 6 months on therapy — the NICE standard for thyroid (and renal) monitoring on lithium.
  • Subclinical on lithium (TSH 5–10, normal free T4): monitor every 4–6 months; many patients stabilize without treatment.
  • TSH ≥ 10 on lithium: start levothyroxine — do not stop the lithium.
  • Any new fatigue, weight gain, cognitive dulling, or depressive slide on lithium: check TSH out of cycle. Lithium depression and lithium hypothyroidism wear the same clothes.

Amiodarone — the other great thyroid disruptor

Amiodarone is an iodine bomb with a months-long half-life. Across studies, roughly 14–18% of patients develop overt thyroid dysfunction on it; a nationwide Icelandic cohort found about 40% had some thyroid dysfunction by 5 years. It breaks the axis both ways: amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism (the gland, flooded with iodine, shuts down — commoner in iodine-replete regions, ~12–17%) and amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT), which comes in two types — type 1, excess hormone synthesis in an abnormal gland (nodular goiter, latent Graves'), and type 2, a destructive thyroiditis that leaks stored hormone. Onset can occur at any point in therapy and even after the drug is stopped. Psych-unit relevance: your patients with cardiac histories arrive on it, it interacts with the QTc-conscious prescribing your unit already does, and a "new anxiety with tachycardia" in an amiodarone patient is a TFT order waiting to happen. Baseline TFTs, an early recheck in the first months, and periodic (≈ 6-monthly) checks during and after therapy are the standard surveillance pattern.

Postpartum — Thyroiditis vs Depression vs Psychosis

Three Diagnoses, One Exhausted Mother

Lead-in: postpartum thyroiditis (PPT) is a painless autoimmune thyroiditis that strikes 5–10% of women in the first year after delivery — and its two phases impersonate the two psychiatric emergencies of the postpartum period in turn.

Phase 1 — thyrotoxic (≈ months 1–4)

The inflamed gland leaks stored hormone: anxiety, irritability, palpitations, tremor, insomnia, weight loss. This is a leak, not overproduction — so antithyroid drugs do not help; beta-blockers cover symptoms. The trap: labeling it "new-mother anxiety" while a destructive thyroiditis runs its course.

Phase 2 — hypothyroid (≈ months 4–8)

The gland, emptied and inflamed, under-produces: fatigue, low mood, poor concentration, weight gain — a presentation that overlaps postpartum depression almost symptom for symptom. The differentiator costs one tube of blood: check TSH in every postpartum depression workup.

  • Course: most women recover thyroid function by about 12 months — but 20–30% develop permanent hypothyroidism, and recurrence in a later pregnancy is about 70%.
  • Risk flag: women with positive anti-TPO in the first trimester develop PPT in roughly a third to a half of cases; the Endocrine Society pattern is to check TSH at 3 and 6 months postpartum in high-risk women.
  • Postpartum psychosis is its own emergency — a bipolar-spectrum crisis, usually within the first two weeks, managed psychiatrically and urgently. Thyroid disease does not explain it away; but a TSH still belongs in its medical workup, because thyroiditis and psychosis can share a calendar.

The Emergency Bookends

Myxedema Coma & Thyroid Storm

❄️ Bookend 1 — Myxedema coma (too little, too long)

The end-stage of severe untreated hypothyroidism — a misnomer (frank coma is not required) and a true ICU emergency with reported mortality of 30–60%. Typically an older woman with known or unknown hypothyroidism, tipped over by a stressor.

  • Presentation: hypothermia, bradycardia, hypotension, hypoventilation (CO₂ retention), hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, ileus, and altered mentation from lethargy to coma; delayed reflex relaxation (the Woltman sign) if reflexes are testable.
  • Precipitants: infection (the commonest), cold exposure, sedatives and opioids, stroke, MI, heart failure, GI bleed, and the quiet one — stopped levothyroxine.
  • Nursing priorities: recognize and escalate early; ICU-level monitoring; airway and ventilatory support; passive rewarming (active external rewarming can vasodilate and collapse the patient); IV levothyroxine per orders — and stress-dose hydrocortisone given with or before the thyroid hormone, because coexisting adrenal insufficiency must be covered before replacement unmasks it; correct hypoglycemia and electrolytes; hunt the precipitant (cultures, antibiotics when infection is suspected); and treat every sedating PRN as a loaded dose — these patients clear sedatives and opioids extremely slowly.

🔥 Bookend 2 — Thyroid storm (too much, too fast)

Decompensated thyrotoxicosis: the mirror emergency. Mortality is 10–30% even with treatment, and 80–100% untreated — the fastest-moving endocrine emergency a psych nurse will meet, and the one most likely to be misread as "just mania" or excited delirium.

  • Presentation: fever (often > 103 °F), marked tachycardia or new AF (frequently > 140), hypertension then collapse, tremor, sweating, vomiting/diarrhea — and CNS signs on a gradient: agitation → delirium/psychosis → seizures → coma.
  • Precipitants: infection, surgery, trauma, iodine loads (contrast studies), DKA, hypoglycemia, abrupt cessation of antithyroid medication — and childbirth.
  • The Burch–Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS) formalizes the bedside picture — points for temperature (99–99.9 °F = 5, rising to ≥ 104 °F = 30), CNS effects (agitation 10 · delirium/psychosis 20 · seizure/coma 30), heart rate (99–109 = 5, rising to ≥ 140 = 25), heart failure, GI/hepatic signs, and a present precipitant (10): ≥ 45 = thyroid storm · 25–44 = impending storm · < 25 = unlikely. A nurse who can tally a BWPS can move a whole team.
  • Treatment sequence (know the order — it is tested and it matters): (1) beta-blocker for rate and adrenergic blockade; (2) a thionamide — propylthiouracil (PTU) is preferred in storm because it also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion; (3) iodine at least 1 hour AFTER the thionamide (iodine first would feed new hormone synthesis); (4) hydrocortisone; (5) aggressive cooling with acetaminophen — never aspirin or NSAIDs, which displace T4 from its binding protein and raise free hormone; (6) ICU, treat the precipitant.
  • Psych-unit pearl: for the agitated storm patient, benzodiazepines are the preferred sedative — and every minute spent assuming the agitation is "behavioral" is a minute the fever climbs. Fever + tachycardia + delirium is a medical code picture until proven otherwise.

Treatment — Levothyroxine & the Long Game

Start Low in the Old & Cardiac · Recheck at 6–8 Weeks

Lead-in: treatment of primary hypothyroidism is replacement with levothyroxine (synthetic T4) — once daily, inexpensive, and titrated against TSH. The psychiatry of it is a timing problem: the lab recovers first, the brain recovers later, and the nurse's job is to keep everyone — patient, family, and treatment team — from declaring failure in the gap.

Dosing anchors

  • Full replacement in a younger adult without cardiac disease ≈ 1.6 mcg/kg/day (about 112 mcg at 70 kg).
  • Elderly or cardiac patients start low: 25–50 mcg daily, increased by 12.5–25 mcg every 6–8 weeks — replacing too fast can provoke angina or arrhythmia in a heart that has run slow for years.
  • Target: TSH back in range — a common practical target is 0.5–4.5 mIU/L, individualized by age and symptoms.
  • Once stable: TSH every 6–12 months, and after any weight change, new interacting drug, or pregnancy.

The recheck rhythm. Levothyroxine's long half-life means steady state takes about six weeks — a TSH drawn at week 2 measures the old dose, not the new one.

Why the mind lags the lab

Free T4 typically normalizes within about 2–3 weeks of a correct dose, but TSH takes a further 3–6 weeks to re-equilibrate — and psychiatric recovery trails both. The standard counsel, and the honest one: give a dose change 6–8 weeks before judging its effect on mood or cognition. Brains remodel slowly; neurotransmitter receptor density, cerebral blood flow, and months of accumulated deconditioning do not reverse on lab time. A patient discharged euthyroid-but-still-flat has not failed treatment — they are mid-course. Document the trajectory, set the outpatient recheck before discharge, and say the quiet part in the discharge teaching: the medicine is working; the feeling follows.

The T3 augmentation note

For treatment-resistant depression, psychiatrists sometimes augment an antidepressant with liothyronine (T3) — usually 25 mcg daily, increased toward 50 mcg — even in patients with normal thyroid function. The anchor evidence is STAR*D level 3, where T3 augmentation produced remission in 24.7% versus 15.9% for lithium augmentation after two failed antidepressants; the difference did not reach statistical significance, but T3 was better tolerated, with fewer patients stopping for side effects — which is why it remains in the APA's augmentation toolkit with moderate clinical confidence. Nursing notes: T3 is short-acting (sympathomimetic side effects — palpitations, anxiety, tremor — are dose signals), it suppresses TSH on labs (expected, not an error), and it is augmentation, never a substitute for treating true hypothyroidism with levothyroxine.

Hashimoto's encephalopathy — the rare zebra

One more pattern for the workup file: Hashimoto's encephalopathy (steroid-responsive encephalopathy with autoimmune thyroiditis) — an acute/subacute presentation mixing psychiatric symptoms with seizures, myoclonus, stroke-like episodes, and very high anti-TPO titers, in a patient whose TSH may be only mildly abnormal. Rare, contested, and treatable with corticosteroids — worth a thought when psychosis arrives with neurological signs that do not fit.

🐢 The hypothyroid pattern

  • Vitals: bradycardia, low-normal or low temperature, sometimes rising diastolic pressure.
  • General: fatigue out of proportion, weight gain, cold intolerance, puffy face/hands (non-pitting).
  • Skin/hair: cool, dry, coarse; thinning hair and outer eyebrows.
  • Neuro: slowed speech and thought, delayed reflex relaxation, poor concentration, hypersomnia.
  • GI/GU: constipation, heavy menses.
  • Mood: depressed, apathetic, anhedonic; occasionally irritable/anxious instead.

🐇 The hyperthyroid pattern

  • Vitals: tachycardia (including in sleep), widened pulse pressure, fever when decompensating.
  • General: restless energy, weight loss with good appetite, heat intolerance, sweating.
  • Skin/hair: warm, moist, smooth; fine hair; tremor of outstretched hands.
  • Eyes: lid lag, stare; exophthalmos in Graves'.
  • Neuro: pressured speech, racing thoughts, brisk reflexes, insomnia.
  • Mood: anxious, panicky, labile; escalates to mania/psychosis at the extreme.

🚩 Red flags — escalate, do not observe

  • Fever + tachycardia + delirium in a "manic" patient → think thyroid storm, tally a Burch–Wartofsky, call it up the chain now.
  • Hypothermia, bradycardia, hard-to-rouse, or hypoventilating in a hypothyroid patient → think myxedema coma.
  • New atrial fibrillation or a resting pulse that stays > 120 despite PRNs → get TFTs and an ECG, not just another PRN.
  • A hypothyroid patient becoming more sedated after routine sedatives/opioids → reduced clearance; reassess dosing and alert the prescriber.
  • A "depression" with a fresh goiter, vitiligo, or a strong autoimmune family history → antibodies and a prescriber conversation.

Patient teaching points

  • Same time, every day, empty stomach: levothyroxine is taken 30–60 minutes before breakfast; consistency matters more than the exact hour.
  • Keep the blockers away: iron, calcium supplements, antacids, and bile-acid resins block absorption — separate them from the levothyroxine dose by several hours.
  • Do not stop it when you feel better — feeling better is the medicine working; stopping is the commonest on-ramp to severe disease.
  • Expect the lag: blood levels improve in weeks; mood and energy follow over 6–8+ weeks. Judge the medicine at the recheck, not at day 10.
  • Supplements count: tell every provider about biotin and "thyroid support" products — one corrupts the test, the other can contain unlisted hormone.
  • Pregnancy changes the dose: anyone on levothyroxine who becomes pregnant needs prompt follow-up — needs rise early.

Why TSH Belongs in Every Psych Admission

One Cheap Test Standing Guard Over Every Expensive Label

Lead-in: every diagnosis on a psychiatric unit is provisional until the medical mimics are cleared, and the thyroid is the mimic with the best effort-to-yield ratio in the building. The admission TSH earns its place five ways:

  • It finds reversible disease. Hypothyroidism is among the most common reversible causes of depression and of a dementia-like picture; hyperthyroidism is a reversible cause of anxiety, mania, and psychosis. No other single tube changes as many treatment plans.
  • It protects the "treatment-resistant" label. Antidepressants underperform in untreated hypothyroidism; a patient can collect two failed trials and an augmentation plan for what is, in part, a TSH of 15. Check the axis before escalating the pharmacy.
  • It is wired into the unit's own medications. Lithium monitoring starts with a baseline TSH; rapid-cycling bipolar disorder demands one; amiodarone histories demand one.
  • It times the postpartum correctly. Postpartum depression and psychosis workups are incomplete without it — thyroiditis shares their calendar and half their symptoms.
  • It catches the emergencies upstream. The storm and the coma both start as an abnormal number on an admission panel that somebody read carefully.

The nurse's specific role: verify the pair (TSH with free T4 when abnormal), ask the three artifact questions (biotin, heparin, acute illness), flag any TSH > 10 for same-day prescriber review, and make sure a scheduled 6–8-week recheck survives the discharge paperwork. On a busy unit, that is the whole job — and it is enough to change outcomes.
